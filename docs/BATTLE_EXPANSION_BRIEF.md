# Battle expansion brief

Prepared 26 October 2026 as the editorial starting point for the expansion described in [BATTLE_EXPANSION_PROMPT.md](BATTLE_EXPANSION_PROMPT.md).

**Status: unverified working notes.** Nothing in this file has been checked against a source opened for this purpose. Every claim must be confirmed against a source that is actually read before it enters `data/research.json`. Where a source disagrees with this brief, the source wins and the brief should be corrected. The "Read" lines name candidate texts and institutions only; they are not citations and carry no URLs.

The briefs follow the existing editorial rules in [SOURCE_AUDIT.md](SOURCE_AUDIT.md): no casualty figures, no invented quotations, no guessed troop coordinates and no numerical commander ratings. Ancient army sizes are left out on purpose.

Each entry gives the proposed slug, kind, campaign name (an existing one where possible), factions in the `A|B` catalog form, linked commanders (profiles marked _new_ do not exist yet) and a topic tag, followed by:

- **Situation**: the strategic problem
- **Plans**: what each side intended
- **Decisive action**: the move that settled it
- **Outcome**: immediate result and consequence
- **Lesson**: the command principle the study should teach
- **Debates**: what the study must mark as `uncertainty`

## 1. Alexander's campaign (war `alexander`)

### Granicus — May 334 BCE

`granicus` · Battle · Macedonian invasion of Persia · `Macedonian-led army|Persian satraps & Greek mercenaries` · Alexander; Memnon of Rhodes _(new)_ · Topic: _River crossing & shock_

- **Situation:** Soon after crossing the Hellespont, Alexander met the assembled satrapal forces of western Asia Minor behind the Granicus river. No Persian king was present, and command was shared among satraps.
- **Plans:** The satraps lined the steep riverbank with cavalry to stop the crossing; their Greek mercenary infantry stood back. Memnon is reported to have advised scorched earth and avoiding battle, and was overruled.
- **Decisive action:** Alexander forced the crossing with cavalry and fought a close mêlée on the bank, where he was nearly killed and was saved by Cleitus. When the Persian cavalry broke, the mercenary infantry was surrounded and cut down.
- **Outcome:** The coastal cities of Asia Minor lay open, though Persian naval power and Memnon's Aegean counteroffensive remained a threat.
- **Lesson:** Personal leadership and speed against a divided command; how a defensive position can be undone by poor sequencing between arms.
- **Debates:** Arrian has an immediate late-afternoon assault; Diodorus has a dawn crossing unopposed. The massacre of the mercenaries and the treatment of Greek prisoners.
- **Read:** Arrian, _Anabasis_ 1.13–16; Diodorus 17.19–21; Plutarch, _Alexander_ 16; Livius.org battle article if one exists.

### Issus — November 333 BCE

`issus` · Battle · Macedonian invasion of Persia · `Macedonian-led army|Achaemenid Persia` · Alexander; Darius III _(new)_ · Topic: _Terrain & position_

- **Situation:** Darius III brought the royal army round behind Alexander through the Amanus passes, cutting his communications on the Cilician coast.
- **Plans:** Darius held the line of the Pinarus between the mountains and the sea, with heavy cavalry massed on the seaward flank and Greek mercenaries in the centre. Alexander had to turn back and attack on a narrow plain.
- **Decisive action:** Alexander led the Companion cavalry and hypaspists against the Persian left, broke it and turned toward Darius in the centre. Darius fled. Meanwhile Parmenion held the left against heavy cavalry pressure and the phalanx struggled crossing the riverbed.
- **Outcome:** Darius's family and baggage were captured at Damascus. The road to Phoenicia and Egypt opened.
- **Lesson:** Narrow ground negated Persian numbers; attacking the enemy commander's position as the centre of gravity.
- **Debates:** Which river was the Pinarus; the size of the Persian army in ancient accounts; whether Darius fled before or after his line collapsed.
- **Read:** Arrian 2.6–11; Curtius 3.8–11; Polybius 12.17–22 (critique of Callisthenes' account).

### Siege of Tyre — January to July/August 332 BCE

`tyre` · Siege · Macedonian invasion of Persia · `Macedonian-led army|City of Tyre` · Alexander · Topic: _Combined land–sea siege_

- **Situation:** Alexander's strategy against the Persian fleet was to take its bases on land. Island Tyre refused him entry.
- **Plans:** Build a mole across the strait for siege towers. Tyre relied on its walls, its fleet and hope of Carthaginian help.
- **Decisive action:** Tyrian fireships and sorties destroyed the first towers. The turning point came when Phoenician and Cypriot squadrons, whose home cities had submitted, joined Alexander and gave him command of the sea. Ship-mounted rams then breached the walls, and a combined assault took the city.
- **Outcome:** The city was sacked; Arrian reports mass killing and enslavement. The Persian fleet disintegrated as its home ports fell.
- **Lesson:** Defeating a navy from the land; engineering persistence; the political dimension of naval allegiance.
- **Debates:** The length of the mole and how the strait was later silted; the scale of the reprisals (ancient figures should not be repeated as fact).
- **Read:** Arrian 2.15–24; Curtius 4.2–4; Diodorus 17.40–46.

### Siege of Gaza — autumn 332 BCE

`gaza-332-bce` · Siege · Macedonian invasion of Persia · `Macedonian-led army|Persian garrison of Gaza` · Alexander · Topic: _Siege engineering_

- **Situation:** Gaza, held by the commander Batis, was the last fortress on the coastal road to Egypt.
- **Plans:** The engineers judged the city too high to assault. Alexander ordered earth mounds raised around the walls to bring siege engines level.
- **Decisive action:** Repeated assaults, mining and the mounds broke the walls after about two months. Alexander was wounded during a sortie.
- **Outcome:** The city fell and its population was killed or enslaved. Egypt submitted without a fight.
- **Lesson:** Prestige and secure communications as reasons to besiege; the effort a modest fortress could impose.
- **Debates:** Curtius's story of Batis dragged behind a chariot (absent from Arrian); the length of the siege.
- **Read:** Arrian 2.25–27; Curtius 4.6.

### Persian Gate — winter 331/330 BCE

`persian-gate` · Battle · Macedonian invasion of Persia · `Macedonian-led army|Persian force under Ariobarzanes` · Alexander · Topic: _Mountain envelopment_

- **Situation:** After Gaugamela, Alexander split his army and drove through the Zagros toward Persepolis. Ariobarzanes walled a defile and blocked it.
- **Plans:** Hold the pass long enough to save Persepolis and its treasury.
- **Decisive action:** A frontal attack failed under missile fire. A captive herdsman showed Alexander a mountain track. He led a picked force round on a night march while Craterus held the camp and attacked on a signal. The defenders were caught between them.
- **Outcome:** Persepolis was taken; the palace was later burned.
- **Lesson:** Reconnaissance and local knowledge; fixing and turning in mountains; the costs of a frontal attack.
- **Debates:** The location of the pass (several candidates in the Zagros); the fate of Ariobarzanes.
- **Read:** Arrian 3.18; Curtius 5.3–4; Diodorus 17.68.

### Jaxartes — 329 BCE

`jaxartes` · Battle · Campaigns in Bactria and Sogdiana · `Macedonian-led army|Saka (Scythian) horsemen` · Alexander · Topic: _Combined arms against horse archers_

- **Situation:** On the empire's northern frontier, steppe horsemen massed across the Jaxartes (Syr Darya) while Sogdiana rose behind Alexander.
- **Plans:** Steppe tactics: harass, avoid close combat and draw pursuers into the open. Alexander needed a decisive local defeat to secure the river line.
- **Decisive action:** Bolt-throwers on the bank covered the crossing. A bait force drew the horsemen in. Cavalry and light infantry then pinned them so the heavy cavalry could charge home.
- **Outcome:** The Saka came to terms. The Sogdian revolt continued for two more years.
- **Lesson:** Field artillery, baiting and combining arms against a mobile opponent; the limits of a tactical victory against an insurgency.
- **Debates:** The location of the crossing near Alexandria Eschate (Khujand); whether Alexander was ill during the fight.
- **Read:** Arrian 4.3–4; Curtius 7.7–9.

### Sogdian Rock — 328/327 BCE

`sogdian-rock` · Siege · Campaigns in Bactria and Sogdiana · `Macedonian-led army|Sogdian defenders` · Alexander · Topic: _Psychological warfare_

- **Situation:** Sogdian leaders sheltered with families and supplies on a snow-covered fortress cliff they believed impregnable.
- **Plans:** The defenders taunted that only soldiers with wings could take it. Alexander offered rewards to volunteer climbers.
- **Decisive action:** Experienced climbers scaled the cliff at night with iron pegs and ropes; some fell to their deaths. When the garrison saw soldiers above them at dawn, it surrendered.
- **Outcome:** Among the captives was Roxana, daughter of Oxyartes, whom Alexander married. Reconciliation with the Sogdian elite followed.
- **Lesson:** Shock and perception over material strength; pairing coercion with political settlement.
- **Debates:** Name and location of the rock and its commander (Arrian and Curtius differ); the date.
- **Read:** Arrian 4.18–19; Curtius 7.11.

### Hydaspes — May 326 BCE

`hydaspes` · Battle · Indian campaign · `Macedonian-led army|Paurava kingdom` · Alexander; Porus _(new)_ · Topic: _Deception & river crossing_

- **Situation:** Porus held the far bank of the monsoon-swollen Hydaspes (Jhelum) with infantry, chariots and war elephants that horses would not face.
- **Plans:** Porus aimed to stop any crossing at the bank. Alexander wore down his vigilance with repeated noisy feints at night, then planned a crossing upstream while Craterus fixed Porus at the main camp.
- **Decisive action:** Under cover of a storm, Alexander crossed upstream at night, initially landing on an island. He defeated a chariot and cavalry force under Porus's son, then fought the main battle. Cavalry drew off and enveloped the Indian horse, and Coenus's detachment struck from behind. The phalanx then fought the elephants at close quarters.
- **Outcome:** Porus was captured and kept as a client ruler. Soon afterwards the army refused to march beyond the Hyphasis.
- **Lesson:** Conditioning the enemy with deception; dividing to cross and concentrating to fight; fighting an unfamiliar weapon.
- **Debates:** The crossing site; whether Porus's son's force was large or small; how far Arrian follows Ptolemy's self-interested account.
- **Read:** Arrian 5.8–19; Plutarch, _Alexander_ 60; Curtius 8.13–14.

## 2. Roman Republic and Empire

### Zama — 202 BCE

`zama` · Battle · Scipio's African campaign · war `punic` · `Roman Republic & Numidian allies|Carthage` · Scipio Africanus; Hannibal · Topic: _Adaptive formations_

- **Situation:** Scipio's landing in Africa forced Carthage to recall Hannibal from Italy. Masinissa's Numidian cavalry joined Scipio, reversing the cavalry balance of Cannae.
- **Plans:** Hannibal led with war elephants and kept his Italian veterans in a third line as a reserve. Scipio arranged his maniples in columns with lanes for the elephants to pass through.
- **Decisive action:** Elephants were funnelled through the lanes or driven onto their own flanks. Laelius and Masinissa drove off the Carthaginian cavalry. In the infantry fight Scipio reformed into a single extended line to meet the veterans, and his cavalry returned to strike their rear.
- **Outcome:** Carthage accepted peace in 201 BCE, losing its fleet and external empire.
- **Lesson:** Learning from defeat; cavalry superiority as the deciding factor; reserves on both sides.
- **Debates:** The precise site; whether Hannibal's cavalry was ordered to withdraw to draw the Roman horse away.
- **Read:** Polybius 15.5–16; Livy 30.29–35.

### Cynoscephalae — 197 BCE

`cynoscephalae` · Battle · Second Macedonian War · war `macedonian-wars` _(new)_ · `Roman Republic & Aetolian League|Antigonid Macedon` · Titus Quinctius Flamininus _(new)_; Philip V _(new)_ · Topic: _Legion versus phalanx_

- **Situation:** In fog over the Thessalian hills, both armies drifted into contact on broken ground.
- **Plans:** Philip had not intended battle there. Flamininus fed troops forward as the encounter grew.
- **Decisive action:** Philip's right phalanx, formed on the high ground, drove back the Roman left. His left was still deploying and was broken by the Roman right, led by elephants. An unnamed military tribune then took about twenty maniples and attacked the victorious Macedonian right from behind.
- **Outcome:** Philip accepted peace and lost his position in Greece. Rome proclaimed the "freedom of the Greeks."
- **Lesson:** Subordinate initiative; the flexibility of the manipular legion against a phalanx on broken ground.
- **Debates:** How general Polybius's legion-versus-phalanx comparison (18.28–32) is; Aetolian claims to credit.
- **Read:** Polybius 18.18–32; Livy 33.6–10.

### Pydna — 22 June 168 BCE

`pydna` · Battle · Third Macedonian War · war `macedonian-wars` · `Roman Republic|Antigonid Macedon` · Lucius Aemilius Paullus _(new)_; Perseus _(new)_ · Topic: _Cohesion & terrain_

- **Situation:** Perseus held a strong position near Pydna. The battle began from a skirmish over a watering place, the day after a lunar eclipse.
- **Plans:** The phalanx advanced and at first drove the legionaries back.
- **Decisive action:** As the phalanx moved over uneven ground, gaps opened in it. Roman units pushed into them and fought the pikemen at close quarters, where the long pike was useless.
- **Outcome:** The Antigonid monarchy ended. Macedon was divided into four republics and later became a province.
- **Lesson:** A formation built for frontal shock loses cohesion in motion over rough ground.
- **Debates:** The Macedonian cavalry's inaction; ancient accounts of the eclipse; how the battle started.
- **Read:** Livy 44.36–42; Plutarch, _Aemilius Paullus_ 16–22; Polybius fragments of book 29.

### Carrhae — 53 BCE

`carrhae` · Battle · Crassus's Parthian expedition · war `roman-parthian` _(new)_ · `Roman Republic|Parthian Empire` · Marcus Licinius Crassus _(new)_; Surena _(new)_ · Topic: _Missile supply & combined arms_

- **Situation:** Crassus sought glory with an invasion of Parthia across open Mesopotamian country, refusing an Armenian offer of a route through the hills.
- **Plans:** Surena paired horse archers with armoured cataphracts and ran a camel train to resupply arrows.
- **Decisive action:** The legions formed a hollow square and were shot at steadily, because the arrows never ran out. A breakout by Publius Crassus was lured away and destroyed. The army retreated to Carrhae, and Crassus was killed during a parley.
- **Outcome:** Legionary standards were lost; their recovery in 20 BCE became an Augustan propaganda theme. The Euphrates frontier rivalry lasted for centuries.
- **Lesson:** Logistics of firepower; the vulnerability of an infantry-heavy army without its own missile troops and cavalry; overconfidence.
- **Debates:** Plutarch's guide-traitor story; the size of the armies; how far Plutarch's narrative is shaped as a moral tale.
- **Read:** Plutarch, _Crassus_ 17–33; Cassius Dio 40.12–27; Encyclopaedia Iranica on Carrhae if available.

### Pharsalus — 9 August 48 BCE

`pharsalus` · Battle · Caesar's civil war · war `roman-civil-wars` _(new)_ · `Caesarian army|Pompeian army` · Julius Caesar; Pompey _(new)_ · Topic: _Hidden reserve_

- **Situation:** After his repulse at Dyrrhachium, Caesar retreated into Thessaly. Pompey, pressed by the senators in his camp, offered battle.
- **Plans:** Pompey anchored his right on the Enipeus river and massed his much stronger cavalry under Labienus on his left to turn Caesar's right.
- **Decisive action:** Caesar foresaw this and posted a fourth line of picked cohorts behind his right. When Labienus's cavalry swept round, these cohorts charged it, routed it, and turned onto the exposed Pompeian left. Caesar then committed his third line.
- **Outcome:** Pompey fled to Egypt and was murdered. Caesar's victory decided the civil war's main phase.
- **Lesson:** Reading the enemy's main effort and placing a reserve to defeat it; cohort-based flexibility.
- **Debates:** Caesar's own account is partisan; Plutarch and Appian report the cohorts aiming at the faces of the horsemen; the battlefield's location relative to the river.
- **Read:** Caesar, _Civil War_ 3.82–99; Plutarch, _Pompey_ 68–72 and _Caesar_ 42–46; Appian, _Civil Wars_ 2.

### Actium — 2 September 31 BCE

`actium` · Battle · War of Actium · war `roman-civil-wars` · `Octavian's forces|Antony & Cleopatra` · Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa _(new)_; Octavian/Augustus _(new)_; Mark Antony _(new)_ · Topic: _Blockade & strategic attrition_

- **Situation:** Antony's fleet and army gathered on the Ambracian Gulf. Agrippa's earlier raids on Antony's supply bases, including Methone, cut his sea communications.
- **Plans:** Hunger, disease and defections wore Antony down. With the army trapped, he tried a breakout by sea.
- **Decisive action:** As the fleets engaged off the gulf mouth, Cleopatra's squadron broke through the line and sailed south, and Antony followed with a few ships. The rest of the fleet was destroyed or surrendered, and the army capitulated within days.
- **Outcome:** Octavian took Egypt the next year and the civil wars ended, opening the principate.
- **Lesson:** The battle was largely decided by the preceding blockade; logistics and morale as decisive factors.
- **Debates:** Was the escape a planned breakout or a flight? Augustan sources shape the story heavily against Cleopatra.
- **Read:** Cassius Dio 50.11–35; Plutarch, _Antony_ 61–68; Velleius Paterculus 2.84–85.

### Teutoburg Forest — September 9 CE

`teutoburg` · Battle · Varus's campaign in Germania · war `roman-germanic` _(new)_ · `Roman army of Germania|Cheruscan-led coalition` · Publius Quinctilius Varus _(new)_; Arminius _(new)_ · Topic: _Intelligence failure & ambush_

- **Situation:** Varus governed a Germania he believed was being pacified. Arminius, a Cheruscan noble, Roman citizen and auxiliary officer, was one of his trusted advisers.
- **Plans:** Arminius reported a rising that drew three legions with their baggage away from their known routes, then struck them on the march.
- **Decisive action:** The column was attacked over several days in forest and marsh, unable to deploy. At Kalkriese, a turf wall along a narrow strip between hill and bog marks one killing ground. Varus took his own life.
- **Outcome:** Three legions (XVII, XVIII and XIX) were lost. After Germanicus's campaigns of 14–16 CE, Rome settled on the Rhine as its frontier.
- **Lesson:** Reliance on a single trusted source; march discipline in hostile terrain; the political roots of military surprise.
- **Debates:** Whether Kalkriese is the main battle site or one episode; how decisive the defeat was for the frontier policy that followed.
- **Read:** Cassius Dio 56.18–24; Velleius Paterculus 2.117–120; Tacitus, _Annals_ 1.60–62; Museum und Park Kalkriese.

### Watling Street — 60 or 61 CE

`watling-street` · Battle · Boudican revolt · war `roman-britain` _(new)_ · `Roman army of Britain|Iceni, Trinovantes & allies` · Gaius Suetonius Paulinus _(new)_; Boudica _(new)_ · Topic: _Choosing the ground_

- **Situation:** After Roman abuses against the Iceni, Boudica's revolt destroyed Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium while the governor was campaigning on Mona (Anglesey).
- **Plans:** Paulinus abandoned London and concentrated what troops he could. He chose a defile with woods behind and open ground in front. The Britons, confident in numbers, brought their families in wagons to watch.
- **Decisive action:** Rome's men held until the attackers came within range, threw volleys of javelins, then advanced in wedges. The wagons behind the British line blocked any retreat.
- **Outcome:** The revolt collapsed and Boudica died soon after. Roman policy in the province was reviewed and became more conciliatory.
- **Lesson:** Terrain that narrows the frontage favours a disciplined smaller force; the risks of a crowd without a line of retreat.
- **Debates:** The location is unknown (Mancetter and other sites are proposed); Tacitus's casualty figures are not credible; the revolt's atrocities on both sides.
- **Read:** Tacitus, _Annals_ 14.29–39 and _Agricola_ 14–16; Cassius Dio 62.1–12.

### Siege of Jerusalem — spring to September 70 CE

`jerusalem-70` · Siege · Judaean campaign of Vespasian and Titus · war `jewish-roman` _(new)_ · `Roman Empire|Judaean defenders` · Titus _(new)_ · Topic: _Siegecraft & internal division_

- **Situation:** The revolt that began in 66 CE had divided the city between rival factions, led by John of Gischala, Simon bar Giora and Eleazar ben Simon, who fought each other until the Romans arrived.
- **Plans:** Titus broke the three city walls in turn with siege works, then encircled the city with a wall to starve it out.
- **Decisive action:** After the Antonia fortress fell, the Temple was burned, and the upper city was taken with a final assault.
- **Outcome:** The Temple was destroyed and the city devastated. Mass killing and enslavement followed; the Arch of Titus celebrated the triumph. Masada fell later.
- **Lesson:** Systematic siegecraft (ramps, circumvallation, famine); how internal division weakens defence. The study must foreground civilian suffering.
- **Debates:** Josephus was an eyewitness in Roman service, and his figures are unreliable; whether Titus intended the Temple's destruction.
- **Read:** Josephus, _Jewish War_ 5–6; Tacitus, _Histories_ 5.1–13.

### Milvian Bridge — 28 October 312

`milvian-bridge` · Battle · Constantine's Italian campaign · war `tetrarchy-civil-wars` _(new)_ · `Constantine's army|Maxentius's army` · Constantine I _(new)_; Maxentius _(new)_ · Topic: _Abandoning a strong position_

- **Situation:** Constantine invaded Italy from Gaul and won at Turin and Verona. Maxentius held Rome, which he had fortified.
- **Plans:** Maxentius could have stood a siege behind the Aurelian walls. Instead he came out and deployed north of the Tiber, with only the bridges behind him.
- **Decisive action:** Constantine's cavalry broke Maxentius's horse. The army was pushed back against the river, the temporary bridge failed or was overwhelmed, and Maxentius drowned.
- **Outcome:** Constantine took the West, disbanded the Praetorian Guard and began favouring Christianity.
- **Lesson:** Giving up a fortified position for open battle with a river behind; the importance of a secure line of retreat.
- **Debates:** The vision and sign accounts (Lactantius, Eusebius) are later religious narratives; the exact bridge arrangements.
- **Read:** Lactantius, _On the Deaths of the Persecutors_ 44; Eusebius, _Life of Constantine_ 1.27–38; _Panegyrici Latini_ 12.

### Adrianople — 9 August 378

`adrianople` · Battle · Gothic War of 376–382 · war `gothic-war` _(new)_ · `Eastern Roman army|Gothic coalition` · Valens _(new)_; Fritigern _(new)_ · Topic: _Reconnaissance & impatience_

- **Situation:** Goths admitted across the Danube in 376 revolted after abuse by Roman officials. Valens marched against them without waiting for his nephew Gratian's western army.
- **Plans:** Scouts reported a smaller Gothic force than there was. The Goths negotiated to buy time while the Greuthungi cavalry under Alatheus and Saphrax returned.
- **Decisive action:** After a long, hot march, Roman units engaged before deployment was complete. The returning Gothic cavalry struck the Roman left. The infantry was compressed until it could not manoeuvre, and Valens was killed.
- **Outcome:** Most of the eastern field army was lost. Theodosius's settlement of 382 placed Goths inside the empire as federates.
- **Lesson:** Faulty intelligence, impatience for glory and fatigue; waiting for concentration versus seizing the moment.
- **Debates:** The older view that this marked a "cavalry revolution" is now largely rejected; Valens's motives; the size of the armies.
- **Read:** Ammianus Marcellinus 31.12–13; Orosius 7.33.

### Catalaunian Plains — 451

`catalaunian-plains` · Battle · Attila's invasion of Gaul · war `hunnic-invasions` _(new)_ · `Western Roman & Visigothic coalition|Attila's Huns & allies` · Flavius Aetius _(new)_; Attila _(new)_; Theodoric I _(new)_ · Topic: _Coalition warfare_

- **Situation:** Attila invaded Gaul and besieged Aurelianum (Orléans). Aetius assembled a coalition of Romans, Visigoths, Alans and others.
- **Plans:** Both sides contested a ridge dominating the field.
- **Decisive action:** The coalition seized the ridge. Theodoric was killed, but the Visigoths drove back their opponents and Attila withdrew into a wagon laager. Aetius did not press the destruction of the Huns.
- **Outcome:** Attila left Gaul but invaded Italy in 452.
- **Lesson:** Holding a coalition together; the political limits on pursuing a defeated enemy.
- **Debates:** The location and exact date; Jordanes wrote a century later from a Gothic perspective; why Aetius let Attila withdraw.
- **Read:** Jordanes, _Getica_ 36–41; Hydatius's chronicle.

## 3. Napoleon's battles (war `napoleonic`; 1793–1802 under new war `french-revolutionary`)

The core list covers the engagements Napoleon commanded in person, plus Toulon where he made his name. Optional entries are marked; they can share a study with a neighbouring battle as a phase.

### Siege of Toulon — September to 19 December 1793

`toulon` · Siege · Siege of Toulon · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Republic|Anglo-Spanish fleet & royalists` · Napoleon (artillery commander); Jacques Dugommier _(new)_ · Topic: _Finding the decisive point_

- **Situation:** Royalists handed Toulon and the French Mediterranean fleet to an Anglo-Spanish force.
- **Plans:** Captain Bonaparte argued that taking the heights over the inner harbour (L'Éguillette and Balaguier, guarded by Fort Mulgrave, the "Little Gibraltar") would make the anchorage untenable and force evacuation, without storming the city.
- **Decisive action:** After batteries were built up and Dugommier took command, Fort Mulgrave was stormed on 17 December. The allied fleet evacuated, burning part of the French fleet.
- **Outcome:** Republican reprisals followed in the city. Bonaparte was promoted to brigadier general.
- **Lesson:** Attack what makes the enemy's position untenable, not the position itself.
- **Debates:** How much later legend inflates Bonaparte's personal role; the scale of the reprisals.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon (napoleon.org) entries; Chandler, _The Campaigns of Napoleon_ part 1.

### 13 Vendémiaire — 5 October 1795 _(optional)_

`vendemiaire` · Battle · Paris insurrection of 1795 · war `french-revolutionary` · `National Convention|Royalist sections of Paris` · Napoleon; Joachim Murat _(new)_ · Topic: _Civil violence & firepower_

- **Summary:** Bonaparte, serving under Barras, defended the Convention against an armed royalist rising. Murat secured cannon from Sablons, and canister fire broke the columns near Saint-Roch. This was urban fire against fellow citizens, not a field battle; it made Bonaparte's career and led to his command in Italy. Include only with careful framing of its political character.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon.

### Montenotte campaign — 11–28 April 1796

`montenotte` · Campaign · Italian campaign of 1796–1797 · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of Italy|Austria & Piedmont-Sardinia` · Napoleon; Johann Peter Beaulieu _(new)_ · Topic: _Central position_

- **Situation:** In his first army command, Bonaparte faced an Austrian army and a Piedmontese army whose lines of retreat diverged.
- **Plans:** Strike the hinge between them, separate them, and defeat each in turn.
- **Decisive action:** At Montenotte (12 April) the French broke an Austrian column, then won at Millesimo and Dego, and turned on the Piedmontese at Mondovì.
- **Outcome:** With the armistice of Cherasco (28 April), Piedmont left the war within about two weeks.
- **Lesson:** Strategy of the central position; speed; the enemy's political divisions as a weakness.
- **Debates:** How much of the plan predated Bonaparte (earlier staff plans); the poor state of the Army of Italy on his arrival.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler, part 3.

### Lodi — 10 May 1796

`lodi` · Battle · Italian campaign of 1796–1797 · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of Italy|Austrian rearguard` · Napoleon · Topic: _Morale & legend_

- **Situation:** Bonaparte crossed the Po at Piacenza to outflank Beaulieu, who retreated over the Adda.
- **Decisive action:** A grenadier column stormed the bridge at Lodi under fire while cavalry forded upstream. The Austrian rearguard withdrew.
- **Outcome:** Milan was entered within days, but Beaulieu's main army escaped. The episode mattered more for Bonaparte's self-image and reputation than for its military result.
- **Lesson:** Tactical boldness against operational effect; the battle as political theatre.
- **Debates:** Who led the storming column; Napoleon's later remark that Lodi convinced him he was destined for greatness, a memoir-era reconstruction.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Castiglione — 5 August 1796

`castiglione` · Battle · Italian campaign of 1796–1797 · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of Italy|Austria` · Napoleon; Dagobert Sigmund von Wurmser _(new)_ · Topic: _Interior lines_

- **Situation:** Wurmser advanced in separate columns on either side of Lake Garda to relieve besieged Mantua.
- **Plans:** Bonaparte gave up the siege, sacrificing his siege train, to concentrate against each column before they could unite.
- **Decisive action:** He beat Quasdanovich's column around Lonato (3 August), then turned on Wurmser at Castiglione. A division arriving from the south hit the Austrian flank and rear.
- **Outcome:** Wurmser retreated into the Tyrol; Mantua was later blockaded again.
- **Lesson:** Interior lines and giving up a secondary objective to win the main one.
- **Debates:** How well the flank attack was coordinated; Augereau's role.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Arcole — 15–17 November 1796

`arcole` · Battle · Italian campaign of 1796–1797 · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of Italy|Austria` · Napoleon; József Alvinczi _(new)_ · Topic: _Persistence in difficult terrain_

- **Situation:** A third Austrian relief army under Alvinczi pressed on Verona.
- **Plans:** Bonaparte crossed the Adige at Ronco to strike the Austrian rear through marshland crossed only by causeways, where numbers could not be brought to bear.
- **Decisive action:** Repeated attacks on the Arcole bridge failed; Bonaparte's personal attempt with a flag became a famous image. On the third day French troops crossed downstream, and a small party sounding trumpets behind the Austrian flank helped persuade them to withdraw.
- **Outcome:** Alvinczi retreated; Mantua stayed isolated.
- **Lesson:** Using terrain to cancel numerical inferiority; persistence over three days.
- **Debates:** The flag episode's later propaganda image; how decisive the trumpet ruse really was.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Rivoli — 14–15 January 1797

`rivoli` · Battle · Italian campaign of 1796–1797 · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of Italy|Austria` · Napoleon; André Masséna _(new)_; Alvinczi · Topic: _Defeating converging columns_

- **Situation:** Alvinczi's fourth relief attempt sent several separated columns converging on the Rivoli plateau held by Joubert.
- **Plans:** The columns had to coordinate across mountain terrain. Bonaparte rode to Rivoli overnight and concentrated Masséna's division there.
- **Decisive action:** Holding the plateau's key point, the French defeated the columns one by one as they arrived; the column that tried to envelop the French was itself trapped. Bonaparte then marched to beat Provera at La Favorita outside Mantua (16 January).
- **Outcome:** Mantua surrendered on 2 February 1797, opening the road to Austria and the Leoben preliminaries.
- **Lesson:** Converging columns are only strong if they arrive together; rapid concentration.
- **Debates:** The timing of Bonaparte's arrival and orders; Joubert's role.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Battle of the Pyramids — 21 July 1798

`pyramids` · Battle · Egyptian and Syrian campaign · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of the Orient|Mamluk beys` · Napoleon; Murad Bey _(new)_ · Topic: _Squares against cavalry_

- **Situation:** Bonaparte landed in Egypt, a province of the Ottoman Empire ruled in practice by Mamluk beys, and marched on Cairo.
- **Plans:** The French formed divisional squares with artillery at the corners. Murad Bey's horsemen relied on charges; there was an entrenched camp at Embabeh.
- **Decisive action:** Mamluk charges broke against the squares; the Embabeh camp was stormed and many fugitives drowned in the Nile.
- **Outcome:** Cairo was occupied. Within days Nelson destroyed the French fleet at Aboukir Bay (1 August), stranding the army.
- **Lesson:** Formations against a cavalry army; a tactical victory overturned by losing the sea.
- **Debates:** The name (fought at Embabeh, far from the pyramids); the colonial character of the expedition.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Siege of Acre — March to May 1799

`acre` · Siege · Egyptian and Syrian campaign · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of the Orient|Ottoman garrison & Royal Navy` · Napoleon; Jezzar Pasha _(new)_; Sidney Smith _(new)_ · Topic: _Sea power & siege_

- **Situation:** Bonaparte invaded Syria to forestall an Ottoman counteroffensive. At Jaffa, captured prisoners were executed and plague broke out.
- **Plans:** Take Acre and open the coastal road north.
- **Decisive action:** British ships under Sidney Smith captured the French siege artillery at sea and supported the garrison, while the émigré engineer Phélippeaux strengthened the defences. Repeated assaults failed. A relief army was beaten at Mount Tabor (16 April) by Kléber and Bonaparte; include this as a phase.
- **Outcome:** The siege was lifted and the army retreated to Egypt through the desert, abandoning the Syrian venture.
- **Lesson:** Sea control shapes land sieges; disease and logistics.
- **Debates:** The Jaffa executions and whether plague victims were poisoned (contested); Bonaparte's later account of his aims.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Aboukir (land battle) — 25 July 1799

`aboukir-1799` · Battle · Egyptian and Syrian campaign · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of the Orient|Ottoman army` · Napoleon; Murat · Topic: _Counterattack against a landing_

- **Summary:** An Ottoman army landed at Aboukir and entrenched. Bonaparte attacked before it could expand. Murat's cavalry broke the line, and many Ottoman soldiers were driven into the sea. Bonaparte then left Egypt secretly in August 1799, leaving Kléber in command, and returned to France for the Brumaire coup.
- **Lesson:** Striking a landing before it consolidates; the political use of victory.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon.

### Marengo — 14 June 1800

`marengo` · Battle · Marengo campaign · war `french-revolutionary` · `French Army of the Reserve|Austria` · Napoleon (as First Consul); Louis Desaix _(new)_; Michael von Melas _(new)_ · Topic: _Reserves & recovery_

- **Situation:** Bonaparte crossed the Great St Bernard Pass into the Austrian rear in Italy. Searching for Melas, he detached troops, including Desaix's division.
- **Plans:** Melas broke out across the Bormida and attacked an outnumbered French army.
- **Decisive action:** By afternoon the French had been pushed back and the Austrians believed they had won. Desaix returned in the late afternoon, and his counterattack, with Marmont's massed guns and a cavalry charge by the younger Kellermann into the flank of the Austrian column, reversed the day. Desaix was killed.
- **Outcome:** With the Convention of Alessandria the Austrians left northern Italy. The victory secured the consulate.
- **Lesson:** The danger of dispersal; the value of a returning reserve and combined arms at the decisive moment.
- **Debates:** Official accounts were rewritten repeatedly to present the battle as planned; who deserves the credit (Desaix, Kellermann).
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Ulm campaign — 25 September to 20 October 1805

`ulm` · Campaign · War of the Third Coalition (existing) · `French Empire|Austria` · Napoleon; Karl Mack von Leiberich _(new)_; Michel Ney _(new)_ · Topic: _Strategic envelopment_

- **Situation:** Austria advanced into Bavaria before its Russian allies arrived. Mack expected the French to come through the Black Forest.
- **Plans:** The Grande Armée's corps marched from the Rhine and Channel coast in a wide wheel north of the Danube, crossing behind Mack and cutting him from Vienna.
- **Decisive action:** Cavalry screened the Black Forest as a feint. Corps converged on the Danube crossings. Ney's attack at Elchingen (14 October) closed a gap, and Mack was surrounded at Ulm.
- **Outcome:** Mack capitulated with most of his army before a major battle was fought, opening the road to Vienna and Austerlitz.
- **Lesson:** Corps system, strategic surprise and a manoeuvre against the enemy's rear; winning a campaign by movement.
- **Debates:** Mack's reputation (his intelligence was poor and his authority divided); Murat's errors.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler, part 7.

### Jena–Auerstedt — 14 October 1806

`jena-auerstedt` · Battle · War of the Fourth Coalition · `French Empire|Prussia & Saxony` · Napoleon; Louis-Nicolas Davout _(new)_; Charles William Ferdinand, Duke of Brunswick _(new)_ · Topic: _Corps independence & pursuit_

- **Situation:** Prussia went to war without waiting for Russia. The French "battalion square" of corps advanced through the Thuringian forest.
- **Plans:** Napoleon believed the main Prussian army stood before him at Jena. Davout's corps was sent north to cut its retreat.
- **Decisive action:** At Jena, Napoleon defeated Hohenlohe's force, the flank guard. At Auerstedt, Davout's single corps met and defeated the main Prussian army under Brunswick, who was mortally wounded. Bernadotte's corps fought at neither.
- **Outcome:** A relentless pursuit destroyed the Prussian army. Berlin was entered; fortresses surrendered.
- **Lesson:** A corps able to fight alone until help arrives; exploitation and pursuit; Napoleon's misreading of the situation.
- **Debates:** Napoleon's bulletin played down Davout; Bernadotte's conduct.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler, part 8.

### Eylau — 7–8 February 1807

`eylau` · Battle · War of the Fourth Coalition · `French Empire|Russia & Prussia` · Napoleon; Levin August von Bennigsen _(new)_; Murat; Davout · Topic: _Attrition in winter_

- **Situation:** A winter campaign in East Prussia against Bennigsen's Russians.
- **Plans:** Napoleon fixed the Russians frontally while Davout and Ney marched on the flanks.
- **Decisive action:** In a snowstorm, Augereau's corps lost direction and was shattered by Russian artillery. Murat's mass cavalry charge restored the centre. Davout's turning attack was checked by the late arrival of L'Estocq's Prussians. Ney arrived in the evening, and the Russians withdrew overnight.
- **Outcome:** Tactically indecisive and costly for both sides; operations paused until spring.
- **Lesson:** Weather and visibility; the role of a cavalry reserve; the limits of the turning movement.
- **Debates:** Whether to call it a draw or a French victory; Napoleon's propaganda after it.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Friedland — 14 June 1807

`friedland` · Battle · War of the Fourth Coalition · `French Empire|Russia` · Napoleon; Jean Lannes _(new)_; Bennigsen · Topic: _River at the back_

- **Situation:** Bennigsen crossed the Alle at Friedland to destroy Lannes's isolated corps.
- **Plans:** Lannes held from early morning while Napoleon brought up the army. The Russians fought in a loop of the river with bridges behind them.
- **Decisive action:** Ney attacked toward Friedland town and the bridges. Sénarmont pushed his artillery forward to close range, firing canister. The bridges burned and the Russian left collapsed.
- **Outcome:** The Treaties of Tilsit followed: alliance with Russia and partition of Prussia.
- **Lesson:** Never fight with a river at your back; offensive massed artillery; a holding action that buys time.
- **Debates:** Bennigsen's reasoning; artillery losses.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Abensberg–Eckmühl — 19–23 April 1809

`eckmuhl` · Campaign · War of the Fifth Coalition · `French Empire & Confederation of the Rhine|Austria` · Napoleon; Archduke Charles of Austria _(new)_; Davout · Topic: _Restoring a dispersed position_

- **Situation:** Austria invaded Bavaria. Before Napoleon arrived, Berthier's confused orders had left Davout isolated near Regensburg.
- **Plans:** Napoleon concentrated, struck the Austrian centre at Abensberg and pursued toward Landshut, then turned north to support Davout.
- **Decisive action:** At Eckmühl (22 April), Davout fixed and the main army hit the Austrian left. Charles withdrew across the Danube at Regensburg.
- **Outcome:** The way to Vienna lay open, but the Austrian army survived intact.
- **Lesson:** Clear command intent; recovering from a staff failure; a victory without destroying the enemy army.
- **Debates:** Napoleon's misreading of where the main Austrian body was during the Landshut pursuit.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Aspern-Essling — 21–22 May 1809

`aspern-essling` · Battle · War of the Fifth Coalition · `French Empire|Austria` · Napoleon; Archduke Charles; Lannes · Topic: _River-crossing logistics_

- **Situation:** Napoleon crossed the Danube below Vienna by way of Lobau island onto the Marchfeld.
- **Plans:** Charles waited until part of the French army was across, then attacked the bridgehead villages of Aspern and Essling.
- **Decisive action:** High water and floating obstacles sent down by the Austrians broke the bridges repeatedly, cutting off reinforcements and ammunition. The villages changed hands many times. Lannes was mortally wounded and Napoleon withdrew to Lobau.
- **Outcome:** Napoleon's first major defeat in personal command. He prepared a better crossing.
- **Lesson:** Engineering and logistics decide river crossings; the enemy may choose the moment of contact.
- **Debates:** Charles's failure to exploit the victory.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Wagram — 5–6 July 1809

`wagram` · Battle · War of the Fifth Coalition · `French Empire & allies|Austria` · Napoleon; Archduke Charles; Davout; Étienne Macdonald _(new)_ · Topic: _Mass and engineering_

- **Situation:** After Aspern-Essling, Napoleon spent six weeks fortifying Lobau and preparing prefabricated bridges.
- **Plans:** Cross at night to an unexpected sector, then fight a deliberate battle with massed forces. Charles planned to envelop the French left while holding the Russbach heights.
- **Decisive action:** The crossing succeeded. On 6 July, Davout turned the Austrian left at Markgrafneusiedl while a great battery and Macdonald's massive column pressed the centre. The Austrians withdrew in good order.
- **Outcome:** The armistice of Znaim and the Treaty of Schönbrunn followed. It was a victory, but showed the growing cost and declining quality of the Grande Armée.
- **Lesson:** Preparation, engineering, the grand battery; the shift toward attrition battles.
- **Debates:** Bernadotte's dismissal; whether the battle marks the end of Napoleon's tactical virtuosity.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Smolensk — 16–18 August 1812

`smolensk-1812` · Battle · Russian campaign of 1812 · `French Empire & allies|Russian Empire` · Napoleon; Michael Barclay de Tolly _(new)_; Pyotr Bagration _(new)_ · Topic: _Failed envelopment_

- **Situation:** The two Russian armies united at Smolensk after evading Napoleon's attempts to defeat them separately.
- **Plans:** Napoleon swung south of the Dnieper (the "Smolensk manoeuvre") to get behind them.
- **Decisive action:** A rearguard at Krasnoi delayed the advance. Smolensk was then assaulted frontally, the city burned, and the Russians withdrew in good order.
- **Outcome:** The pursuit continued deeper into Russia; the decisive battle Napoleon wanted was postponed.
- **Lesson:** Distance, supply and a retreating enemy who refuses to be fixed.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Borodino — 7 September 1812

`borodino` · Battle · Russian campaign of 1812 · `French Empire & allies|Russian Empire` · Napoleon; Mikhail Kutuzov; Bagration · Topic: _Attrition & the uncommitted reserve_

- **Situation:** Kutuzov made a stand before Moscow behind field fortifications: the Bagration flèches and the Raevsky redoubt.
- **Plans:** Davout proposed a wide flanking march round the Russian left; Napoleon chose frontal assaults.
- **Decisive action:** In hours of attrition the French took the flèches and then the great redoubt. Bagration was mortally wounded. Napoleon declined to commit the Imperial Guard.
- **Outcome:** The Russians withdrew and the French entered Moscow on 14 September, but no peace followed. The army began its retreat in October.
- **Lesson:** A battle won without winning the campaign; when to commit the last reserve; the rejected flanking alternative.
- **Debates:** Napoleon's illness and passivity during the battle; the Guard decision; casualty figures are disputed and should not be quoted.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler; Tolstoy is fiction, not a source.

### Maloyaroslavets — 24 October 1812 _(optional)_

`maloyaroslavets` · Battle · Russian campaign of 1812 · Eugène de Beauharnais's corps against Dokhturov · Topic: _A decision to retreat_

- **Summary:** Leaving Moscow, Napoleon tried to retreat by a southern route through unravaged country. Eugène's corps took the burning town after repeated fighting, but Kutuzov blocked the road beyond. Napoleon chose to return to the devastated Smolensk road, a decision that shaped the catastrophe of the retreat. This works best as a phase of a "Retreat from Moscow" study with the Berezina.

### Berezina — 26–29 November 1812

`berezina` · Battle · Russian campaign of 1812 · `French Empire & allies|Russian Empire` · Napoleon; Jean-Baptiste Eblé _(new)_; Pavel Chichagov _(new)_ · Topic: _Engineers & escape_

- **Situation:** The remnant of the Grande Armée reached the Berezina to find the Borisov bridge destroyed and Russian armies converging.
- **Plans:** A feint downstream drew Chichagov south while Eblé's pontonniers built two trestle bridges at Studienka, working in icy water.
- **Decisive action:** Oudinot's and Victor's corps held both banks while the core of the army crossed. The bridges were burned on the morning of 29 November, leaving many stragglers and civilians behind.
- **Outcome:** The army escaped destruction, but as a fighting force it was finished.
- **Lesson:** Deception, engineering and rearguard action under catastrophic conditions; the human cost of retreat.
- **Debates:** Russian coordination failures; the order to burn the bridges.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Lützen — 2 May 1813

`lutzen-1813` · Battle · German campaign of 1813 · `French Empire|Russia & Prussia` · Napoleon; Peter Wittgenstein _(new)_; Gerhard von Scharnhorst _(new)_ · Topic: _Rebuilt army, no cavalry_

- **Situation:** Napoleon raised a new army of conscripts after 1812. The Allies attacked Ney's corps around Grossgörschen.
- **Decisive action:** Ney's young troops held the villages while Napoleon wheeled the army onto the Allied flank. The Guard's attack and massed artillery decided the day; Scharnhorst was mortally wounded.
- **Outcome:** The Allies withdrew, but a lack of cavalry prevented any pursuit.
- **Lesson:** Without cavalry, victories cannot be exploited; how quickly an army can be rebuilt.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Bautzen — 20–21 May 1813

`bautzen` · Battle · German campaign of 1813 · `French Empire|Russia & Prussia` · Napoleon; Ney · Topic: _Coordination failure_

- **Situation:** The Allies held a fortified position on the Spree.
- **Plans:** Napoleon pinned them frontally while Ney's wing swung round the Allied right to cut their retreat.
- **Decisive action:** Ney, unclear about his objective, turned toward the fighting instead of driving deep behind the Allies. The envelopment closed too late, and the Allies escaped.
- **Outcome:** The armistice of Pläswitz followed (June to August). Austria then joined the coalition.
- **Lesson:** An envelopment depends on clear intent to subordinates; a victory without a decision.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Dresden — 26–27 August 1813

`dresden` · Battle · German campaign of 1813 · `French Empire|Austria, Russia & Prussia` · Napoleon; Karl Philipp zu Schwarzenberg _(new)_ · Topic: _Interior lines & the Trachenberg plan_

- **Situation:** Under the Trachenberg plan, the Allies avoided battle with Napoleon in person and attacked his subordinates. Schwarzenberg's Army of Bohemia advanced on Dresden.
- **Decisive action:** Napoleon returned at speed, held the city, and on the second day enveloped the Austrian left in rain. The Allies retreated.
- **Outcome:** A clear victory, but Vandamme's pursuit corps was destroyed at Kulm (29–30 August), and Napoleon's marshals lost at Grossbeeren, the Katzbach and Dennewitz.
- **Lesson:** A coalition strategy that refuses battle with the strongest commander wins by defeating his subordinates.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Leipzig — 16–19 October 1813

`leipzig` · Battle · German campaign of 1813 · `French Empire & allies|Austria, Prussia, Russia & Sweden` · Napoleon; Schwarzenberg; Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher _(new)_; Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte _(new)_ · Topic: _Convergence of armies_

- **Situation:** Four Allied armies converged on Napoleon at Leipzig. This "Battle of the Nations" was the largest European battle before the 20th century.
- **Decisive action:** Napoleon attacked Schwarzenberg on 16 October without decisive result while Blücher pressed from the north. Saxon and Württemberg troops changed sides. During the retreat on the 19th, the Elster bridge was blown too early, trapping the rearguard; Poniatowski drowned.
- **Outcome:** Germany east of the Rhine was lost and the Confederation of the Rhine collapsed.
- **Lesson:** Converging armies and the failure of interior lines once the enemy is everywhere; coalition politics on the battlefield.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Hanau — 30–31 October 1813 _(optional)_

`hanau` · Battle · German campaign of 1813 · Napoleon against Karl Philipp von Wrede's Bavarian-Austrian force · Topic: _Breaking a blocking force_

- **Summary:** Bavaria had changed sides. Wrede tried to block the French retreat to the Rhine at Hanau with a river behind him. Drouot's Guard artillery and cavalry broke through, and the army escaped to France. A good short study of a poorly chosen blocking position.

### Brienne and La Rothière — 29 January and 1 February 1814 _(optional)_

`la-rothiere` · Battle · Campaign in France, 1814 · Napoleon against Blücher and Schwarzenberg · Topic: _Outnumbered defence_

- **Summary:** Napoleon surprised Blücher at Brienne, where he had attended military school, but was defeated by the combined Allies at La Rothière, his first defeat on French soil. This sets up the Six Days' campaign.

### Six Days' campaign — 10–15 February 1814

`six-days-campaign` · Campaign · Campaign in France, 1814 · `French Empire|Prussia & Russia` · Napoleon; Blücher · Topic: _Defeat in detail_

- **Situation:** The Allies advanced on Paris in two separate armies. Blücher's Army of Silesia was strung out along the Marne valley.
- **Plans:** Strike Blücher's dispersed corps in turn before Schwarzenberg could help.
- **Decisive action:** In quick succession Napoleon won at Champaubert (10 February), Montmirail (11), Château-Thierry (12) and Vauchamps (14).
- **Outcome:** Brilliant operational results that could not change the strategic balance. Montereau (18 February) followed against Schwarzenberg _(optional phase)_.
- **Lesson:** Defeat in detail at its best, and why it is not enough when the enemy has more armies than you have time.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Craonne and Laon — 7–10 March 1814

`laon` · Battle · Campaign in France, 1814 · `French Empire|Prussia & Russia` · Napoleon; Blücher · Topic: _Attrition of the last army_

- **Summary:** At Craonne, Napoleon won a costly victory against a Russian rearguard on a plateau. At Laon he attacked Blücher's concentrated army in a strong position. Marmont's corps was surprised and routed at Athies in a night attack. The French withdrew, unable to spare the losses.
- **Lesson:** Repeated offensives with dwindling forces; the danger of a lax night camp.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Arcis-sur-Aube — 20–21 March 1814

`arcis-sur-aube` · Battle · Campaign in France, 1814 · `French Empire|Austria & allies` · Napoleon; Schwarzenberg · Topic: _Strategic misjudgement_

- **Situation:** Napoleon struck at what he thought was Schwarzenberg's rearguard and met the main Allied army.
- **Decisive action:** After two days of fighting he disengaged across the Aube. He then moved east against the Allied communications, hoping to draw them away from Paris.
- **Outcome:** Instead, the Allies, knowing Paris's mood from intercepted letters, marched on the capital. Paris capitulated on 31 March and Napoleon abdicated in April.
- **Lesson:** A manoeuvre against communications fails when the enemy aims at the political centre.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; Chandler.

### Ligny — 16 June 1815

`ligny` · Battle · Waterloo campaign (existing) · `French Army of the North|Prussia` · Napoleon; Blücher; Ney; August von Gneisenau _(new)_ · Topic: _Separating the allies_

- **Situation:** Napoleon struck between Wellington's and Blücher's armies in Belgium to defeat them separately.
- **Plans:** Napoleon attacked the Prussians at Ligny while Ney held Wellington at Quatre Bras (include as a phase).
- **Decisive action:** Village fighting wore down the Prussian line until the Guard broke the centre in the evening. Blücher was unhorsed and trapped beneath his horse. D'Erlon's corps marched back and forth between the two battles without fighting in either.
- **Outcome:** The Prussians were beaten but not routed. Gneisenau retreated north to Wavre, not east toward Prussia, which kept them in reach of Waterloo two days later.
- **Lesson:** Defeat is not destruction; the line of retreat as a strategic decision; how lost orders (d'Erlon) cost a decision.
- **Debates:** Who was responsible for the d'Erlon episode; Grouchy's late pursuit.
- **Read:** Fondation Napoléon; National Army Museum; Chandler.

## 4. First World War (war `ww1`)

### First Battle of the Marne — 5–12 September 1914

`marne-1914` · Battle · Western Front in 1914 · `France & Britain|German Empire` · Joseph Joffre _(new)_; Helmuth von Moltke the Younger _(new)_; Ferdinand Foch · Topic: _Command, control & the open flank_

- **Situation:** The German right wing advanced through Belgium toward Paris. The French had lost the Battle of the Frontiers.
- **Plans:** Joffre moved troops west by rail and formed a new army, Maunoury's Sixth, near Paris. Kluck's First Army turned east of Paris to chase the retreating French, exposing its flank.
- **Decisive action:** The Sixth Army attacked the German flank on the Ourcq. Kluck turned to meet it, opening a gap with Bülow's Second Army. The BEF and Franchet d'Espèrey's Fifth Army advanced into the gap. After Lieutenant Colonel Hentsch's mission from headquarters, the Germans retreated to the Aisne.
- **Outcome:** The war of movement in the west ended, then the "race to the sea" and the trench line.
- **Lesson:** Rail mobility, an army commander's calm, and how distant headquarters lose control of a fast campaign.
- **Debates:** The "Schlieffen Plan" debate (how fixed a plan it really was); Hentsch's authority; the Paris taxi legend's scale.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; National Army Museum; Encyclopaedia _1914-1918-online_.

### First Ypres — 19 October to 22 November 1914

`first-ypres` · Battle · Western Front in 1914 · `Britain, France & Belgium|German Empire` · Foch; John French _(new)_; Erich von Falkenhayn _(new)_ · Topic: _Last war of movement_

- **Situation:** At the end of the race to the sea, both sides tried to turn the open northern flank.
- **Decisive action:** The Belgians flooded the Yser plain. The old regular BEF and French reinforcements, coordinated by Foch, held a salient around Ypres against repeated German attacks, including newly raised reserve corps of young volunteers at Langemarck.
- **Outcome:** The Ypres salient was kept and the old BEF was largely destroyed. The front froze from the Channel to Switzerland.
- **Lesson:** Defence by firepower and improvisation; how mobile war becomes trench war.
- **Debates:** The German "Langemarck myth" of student volunteers.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; CWGC; _1914-1918-online_.

### Second Ypres — 22 April to 25 May 1915

`second-ypres` · Battle · Western Front in 1915 · `Britain, Canada & France|German Empire` · Falkenhayn · Topic: _New weapons without exploitation_

- **Situation:** German forces prepared the first large-scale chlorine release on the Western Front.
- **Decisive action:** Released from cylinders on 22 April, the gas broke French colonial and territorial divisions and opened a gap. Canadian troops and improvised reserves held its edges. German forces had not been given the reserves to exploit a breakthrough.
- **Outcome:** The salient shrank but held. Gas warfare spread to both sides, along with respirators.
- **Lesson:** Technological surprise is wasted without a plan and reserves to exploit it; the legal and moral dimension under the Hague Conventions.
- **Read:** Canadian War Museum; Imperial War Museums; _1914-1918-online_.

### Gallipoli — February 1915 to January 1916

`gallipoli` · Campaign · Dardanelles campaign · `British Empire & France|Ottoman Empire` · Ian Hamilton _(new)_; Otto Liman von Sanders _(new)_; Mustafa Kemal Atatürk · Topic: _Joint and amphibious operations_

- **Situation:** The Allies tried to force the Dardanelles, knock the Ottoman Empire out of the war and open a supply route to Russia.
- **Plans:** First a naval assault (18 March), which failed in an undetected minefield, then landings at Helles and Anzac Cove (25 April).
- **Decisive action:** Ottoman defenders, with Mustafa Kemal's counterattacks on the heights, confined the landings to shallow beachheads. The August offensive (Sari Bair, Chunuk Bair) and the Suvla landing lost their chance through slow command and exhaustion.
- **Outcome:** The Allies evacuated in December 1915 and January 1916 without loss, the best-executed phase. The campaign became central to Turkish, Australian and New Zealand national memory.
- **Lesson:** The naval assault gave warning before the landings; joint planning, intelligence, logistics and the speed of local command.
- **Debates:** Whether the naval attack was close to success; Hamilton's leadership; competing national narratives.
- **Read:** Australian War Memorial; Imperial War Museums; New Zealand History (nzhistory.govt.nz); _1914-1918-online_.

### Jutland — 31 May to 1 June 1916

`jutland` · Battle · War in the North Sea · `Britain|German Empire` · John Jellicoe _(new)_; David Beatty _(new)_; Reinhard Scheer _(new)_; Franz Hipper _(new)_ · Topic: _Information at sea_

- **Situation:** Scheer hoped to trap part of the British Grand Fleet. Room 40 code-breaking warned of the sortie, but some intelligence was misread.
- **Plans:** Hipper's battlecruisers were to lure Beatty's battlecruisers onto the German battle fleet. Jellicoe's main fleet sailed in support.
- **Decisive action:** Beatty lost battlecruisers to magazine explosions, then turned north and drew the Germans onto Jellicoe. Jellicoe's deployment twice crossed the German "T". Scheer escaped with emergency turn-aways and, at night, through the rear of the British fleet.
- **Outcome:** Britain lost more ships, but the strategic balance was unchanged. The German fleet did not seek a full action again, and Germany turned to unrestricted submarine warfare.
- **Lesson:** Signalling and reporting failures; ammunition-handling and design flaws; risk management for a fleet that must not lose.
- **Debates:** The Jellicoe–Beatty controversy; who "won."
- **Read:** National Museum of the Royal Navy; Imperial War Museums; Naval History and Heritage Command.

### Somme — 1 July to 18 November 1916

`somme` · Battle · Western Front in 1916 (existing) · `Britain, Dominions & France|German Empire` · Douglas Haig; Henry Rawlinson _(new)_; Foch · Topic: _Learning curve & attrition_

- **Situation:** A planned Anglo-French offensive was reshaped by Verdun: the French share shrank and the battle also aimed to relieve Verdun.
- **Plans:** A week-long bombardment was meant to destroy wire and defenders so infantry could walk through.
- **Decisive action:** On 1 July the bombardment had not cut the wire or reached deep dugouts. The British suffered their worst day in history, while the French advanced well to the south. A long attritional campaign followed. Tanks appeared for the first time on 15 September at Flers–Courcelette.
- **Outcome:** Limited ground gained at enormous cost on both sides, with pressure taken off Verdun. German forces later withdrew to the Hindenburg Line.
- **Lesson:** Artillery-infantry coordination; the British army's learning process; Haig's breakthrough ambitions against bite-and-hold.
- **Debates:** "Lions led by donkeys" versus the "learning curve" school; casualty figures must not be quoted without a source.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; CWGC; National Army Museum; _1914-1918-online_.

### Brusilov offensive — 4 June to 20 September 1916

`brusilov-offensive` · Operation · Eastern Front in 1916 · `Russian Empire|Austria-Hungary & German Empire` · Aleksei Brusilov; Conrad von Hötzendorf _(new)_ · Topic: _Broad-front surprise_

- **Situation:** Russia was asked to relieve Allied pressure at Verdun and in Italy.
- **Plans:** Instead of one massed thrust, Brusilov attacked on a broad front with all four armies of his Southwestern Front. He used short, accurate bombardments and assault trenches dug close to the enemy line, so reserves could not be concentrated against one breakthrough.
- **Decisive action:** Austro-Hungarian defences around Lutsk collapsed and many prisoners were taken.
- **Outcome:** It was the most successful Allied offensive of the war's middle years. German reinforcements stabilised the front, Romania entered the war, and Russian losses and exhaustion fed into 1917.
- **Lesson:** Surprise, preparation and breadth over massed concentration; how a lack of follow-up reserves limited success.
- **Read:** _1914-1918-online_; Encyclopaedia Britannica.

### Caporetto — 24 October to 19 November 1917

`caporetto` · Battle · Italian Front · `Austria-Hungary & German Empire|Kingdom of Italy` · Otto von Below _(new)_; Luigi Cadorna _(new)_; Erwin Rommel · Topic: _Infiltration & defence in depth_

- **Situation:** After eleven Isonzo battles, Austria-Hungary asked for German help.
- **Plans:** A short, intense bombardment including gas, then infiltration along valleys by small units bypassing strongpoints.
- **Decisive action:** Italian positions, deployed too far forward and without depth, were cut off. Units like Rommel's mountain detachment on Monte Matajur seized key heights, and the line collapsed.
- **Outcome:** A retreat to the Piave, where the Italians held. Cadorna was replaced by Diaz. Allied reinforcements and the Supreme War Council followed.
- **Lesson:** Infiltration tactics; the danger of rigid forward defence; how morale and command climate shape collapse.
- **Debates:** Cadorna's blaming of his own troops; the role of gas.
- **Read:** _1914-1918-online_; Encyclopaedia Britannica.

### Passchendaele (Third Ypres) — 31 July to 10 November 1917

`passchendaele` · Battle · Western Front in 1917 · `Britain, Dominions & France|German Empire` · Haig; Hubert Gough _(new)_; Herbert Plumer _(new)_; Arthur Currie _(new)_ · Topic: _Bite and hold versus breakthrough_

- **Situation:** Haig sought a breakout from Ypres toward the Belgian coast and its U-boat bases.
- **Plans:** Gough's broad, deep objectives failed in August. Plumer then mounted limited "bite and hold" attacks (Menin Road, Polygon Wood, Broodseinde), which succeeded.
- **Decisive action:** Heavy rain and shelling destroyed drainage and turned the ground to mud. The Canadian Corps took Passchendaele village in November.
- **Outcome:** Modest gains at very heavy cost, and a symbol of the war's suffering.
- **Lesson:** Limited, artillery-led objectives work; persisting beyond the point of success does not.
- **Debates:** Haig's reasons for continuing; casualty figures disputed.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; CWGC; Canadian War Museum; _1914-1918-online_.

### Cambrai — 20 November to 7 December 1917

`cambrai-1917` · Battle · Western Front in 1917 · `Britain|German Empire` · Julian Byng _(new)_ · Topic: _Surprise & combined arms_

- **Situation:** A sector of the Hindenburg Line on firm, unshelled ground suited for tanks.
- **Plans:** Massed tanks without a long preliminary bombardment, plus artillery firing by calculation ("predicted fire") without registering on targets, preserving surprise.
- **Decisive action:** On the first day the attack broke deep into the defences. It stalled at Flesquières and through lack of reserves, and cavalry exploitation failed. On 30 November the German counterattack, using infiltration methods, recovered most of the ground.
- **Outcome:** Tactically inconclusive, but it pointed to the 1918 combined-arms methods.
- **Lesson:** Surprise and combined arms can break in; breaking out needs reserves and mobility.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; Tank Museum; _1914-1918-online_.

### German Spring Offensive (Operation Michael) — 21 March to 5 April 1918

`operation-michael` · Operation · Western Front in 1918 · `German Empire|Britain & France` · Erich Ludendorff _(new)_; Foch; Hubert Gough · Topic: _Tactics without strategy_

- **Situation:** After Russia left the war, Germany had a window before American troops arrived in strength.
- **Plans:** A short hurricane bombardment (Bruchmüller's fire plan), then stormtroops infiltrating weak points.
- **Decisive action:** In fog, the British Fifth Army's line broke and the advance went deeper than any on the Western Front since 1914. Amiens was not reached, and the advance outran its supply.
- **Outcome:** At Doullens (26 March) Foch was made coordinating Allied commander. Later German offensives (Georgette, Blücher, Gneisenau, Marne-Reims) used up Germany's best troops.
- **Lesson:** Tactical brilliance without an operational objective; Ludendorff's choice to go where it was succeeding rather than where it mattered.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; National Army Museum; _1914-1918-online_.

### Amiens — 8–12 August 1918

`amiens-1918` · Battle · Western Front in 1918 · `Britain, Dominions & France|German Empire` · Rawlinson; Currie; John Monash _(new)_; Haig; Foch · Topic: _All-arms battle_

- **Situation:** After the German offensives were spent, the Allies turned to the offensive.
- **Plans:** Total secrecy in moving the Canadian Corps, massed tanks, predicted fire, air support, and Australian and Canadian assault formations.
- **Decisive action:** The first day broke the German line wide open and took large numbers of prisoners. Ludendorff called it the "black day" of the German army.
- **Outcome:** The beginning of the Hundred Days offensives that ended the war.
- **Lesson:** Integrating infantry, artillery, armour and aircraft; halting before diminishing returns and switching sectors.
- **Read:** Australian War Memorial; Canadian War Museum; Imperial War Museums.

### Megiddo — 19–25 September 1918

`megiddo-1918` · Battle · Sinai and Palestine campaign · `British Empire & Arab Northern Army|Ottoman Empire & Germany` · Edmund Allenby _(new)_; Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (Seventh Army); Liman von Sanders · Topic: _Deception & cavalry exploitation_

- **Situation:** In Palestine, Ottoman armies held a line from the coast to the Jordan.
- **Plans:** Allenby used dummy camps and horse lines in the Jordan valley to suggest an attack inland, then concentrated in secret on the coast.
- **Decisive action:** Infantry broke the coastal front. The Desert Mounted Corps rode through the gap and passes behind the Ottoman Seventh and Eighth Armies. Aircraft attacked the retreating columns, and Arab forces cut the railway at Deraa.
- **Outcome:** The Ottoman armies in Palestine were destroyed, Damascus fell, and the Armistice of Mudros followed.
- **Lesson:** Deception plus exploitation; mobile warfare in a theatre with space.
- **Debates:** The role of the Arab Revolt and later political promises.
- **Read:** Australian War Memorial; Imperial War Museums; _1914-1918-online_.

### Meuse-Argonne — 26 September to 11 November 1918

`meuse-argonne` · Operation · Western Front in 1918 · `United States & France|German Empire` · John J. Pershing _(new)_ · Topic: _Inexperience and logistics_

- **Situation:** The American Expeditionary Forces' largest battle, part of Foch's general offensive. It came only two weeks after Saint-Mihiel.
- **Plans:** Break through the Hindenburg Line defences in difficult forest and ridge country to cut the German railway at Sedan.
- **Decisive action:** Early gains stalled through congested roads, inexperienced divisions and staff problems. After reorganisation, a renewed offensive broke through in November.
- **Outcome:** It contributed to Germany's collapse and armistice, and remains one of the deadliest battles in US history.
- **Lesson:** Rapidly expanding an army; logistics and staff work; learning under fire.
- **Read:** American Battle Monuments Commission; US Army Center of Military History.

## 5. Second World War (war `ww2`)

### Invasion of Poland — 1 September to 6 October 1939

`poland-1939` · Campaign · Invasion of Poland · `Germany & Slovakia, then the Soviet Union|Poland` · Gerd von Rundstedt _(new)_; Fedor von Bock _(new)_; Edward Rydz-Śmigły _(new)_ · Topic: _Encirclement from all sides_

- **Situation:** Poland, with long borders open on three sides, hoped France and Britain would attack in the west.
- **Plans:** German army groups attacked from East Prussia, Pomerania, Silesia and Slovakia to encircle the Polish army west of the Vistula.
- **Decisive action:** The Polish counterattack on the Bzura was the largest battle of the campaign. The Soviet invasion from the east on 17 September ended any hope of a stand in the southeast. Warsaw capitulated after bombardment.
- **Outcome:** Poland was partitioned, followed by occupation, mass murder of Polish elites and Jews, and Soviet deportations.
- **Lesson:** Concentrated mechanised forces and air power; how geography and alliance failure shape a defence.
- **Debates:** The "Blitzkrieg" label was applied later; the myth of cavalry charging tanks.
- **Read:** National WWII Museum; USHMM; Polish History Museum.

### Battle of France — 10 May to 25 June 1940

`france-1940` · Campaign · Western campaign of 1940 · `Germany|France, Britain, Belgium & Netherlands` · Erich von Manstein _(new)_; Heinz Guderian _(new)_; Maurice Gamelin _(new)_; Gerd von Rundstedt · Topic: _Operational surprise_

- **Situation:** The Allies expected a German drive through central Belgium and planned to advance to meet it (the Dyle Plan).
- **Plans:** The Manstein plan drew the Allies forward into Belgium, then sent the panzer mass through the Ardennes to the Meuse.
- **Decisive action:** At Sedan on 13 May, Guderian's corps crossed the Meuse with concentrated air support. The panzers reached the Channel at Abbeville on 20 May, cutting off the northern armies. A halt order on 24 May and the Dunkirk evacuation (include as a phase) let the British and many French troops escape.
- **Outcome:** France signed an armistice and was occupied and divided.
- **Lesson:** Attacking the seam; tempo against decision cycles; how Allied command and doctrine failed more than equipment.
- **Debates:** Who gave the halt order and why; Allied tank numbers and quality.
- **Read:** National WWII Museum; Imperial War Museums; French Ministry of the Armed Forces.

### Battle of Britain — 10 July to 31 October 1940

`battle-of-britain` · Campaign · Battle of Britain · `United Kingdom|Germany` · Hugh Dowding _(new)_; Keith Park _(new)_; Hermann Göring _(new)_ · Topic: _Integrated air defence_

- **Situation:** Before an invasion (Operation Sea Lion) could be launched, Germany needed air superiority.
- **Plans:** The Luftwaffe attacked shipping, then airfields and radar, then switched to London from 7 September. The RAF relied on the Dowding system: radar, Observer Corps, filter rooms, sector control.
- **Decisive action:** Fighter Command conserved its strength and kept fighting. The switch to London relieved pressure on the airfields. Large raids on 15 September showed the RAF was undefeated.
- **Outcome:** Sea Lion was postponed indefinitely; the night Blitz continued.
- **Lesson:** Networked command and control; intelligence misestimates (Luftwaffe overstated RAF losses); attrition of pilots.
- **Debates:** How close the RAF came to collapse; the role of the Royal Navy in deterrence.
- **Read:** RAF Museum; Imperial War Museums; National Archives.

### Operation Barbarossa — 22 June to early December 1941

`barbarossa` · Operation · German invasion of the Soviet Union · `Germany & Axis allies|Soviet Union` · Fedor von Bock; Rundstedt; Georgy Zhukov · Topic: _Overreach & war of annihilation_

- **Situation:** Hitler's invasion aimed at destroying the Soviet state and at racial conquest. Stalin had dismissed warnings of attack.
- **Plans:** Three army groups (North, Centre, South) were to destroy the Red Army in encirclements near the border.
- **Decisive action:** Huge encirclements at Białystok–Minsk, Smolensk, Kiev and Vyazma–Bryansk. Hitler diverted forces south to Kiev in August. The Red Army kept raising new armies. Supply on a different rail gauge, autumn mud and winter slowed the advance.
- **Outcome:** The Wehrmacht failed to end the war in one campaign. The invasion was accompanied by mass murder, including the start of the Holocaust by shooting, and the death by starvation of Soviet prisoners of war.
- **Lesson:** Strategic overreach, logistics and underestimating the enemy's capacity to regenerate. The study must foreground atrocities.
- **Debates:** The Kiev diversion; any "preventive war" claim is propaganda.
- **Read:** USHMM; National WWII Museum; Imperial War Museums.

### Battle of Moscow — October 1941 to January 1942

`moscow-1941` · Battle · German invasion of the Soviet Union · `Soviet Union|Germany` · Zhukov; Bock · Topic: _Culmination point_

- **Situation:** Operation Typhoon, the drive on Moscow, began with huge encirclements at Vyazma and Bryansk.
- **Plans:** Germany needed to take Moscow before winter. The Soviets fed in reserves while Zhukov reorganised the defence.
- **Decisive action:** The autumn mud and then the frost exhausted the German advance near the city. On 5–6 December a Soviet counteroffensive, including divisions moved from the Far East, threw back Army Group Centre.
- **Outcome:** The first major German defeat of the war. Hitler's "stand fast" order and the dismissal of generals followed.
- **Lesson:** An offensive's culmination point; reserves; intelligence (reports that Japan would not attack in Siberia).
- **Debates:** Weather as excuse versus Soviet resistance as cause; the influence of the Sorge intelligence.
- **Read:** National WWII Museum; Imperial War Museums.

### Attack on Pearl Harbor — 7 December 1941

`pearl-harbor` · Operation · Japanese offensive of 1941–1942 · `Japan|United States` · Isoroku Yamamoto; Chūichi Nagumo _(new)_; Husband Kimmel _(new)_ · Topic: _Tactical success, strategic error_

- **Situation:** Facing an oil embargo, Japan planned a southern offensive and needed the US Pacific Fleet neutralised for a time.
- **Plans:** Yamamoto's carrier strike from the north Pacific, with surprise.
- **Decisive action:** Two waves of carrier aircraft hit battleships and airfields. The US carriers were at sea; fuel tanks, repair yards and submarine bases were not seriously damaged.
- **Outcome:** The United States entered the war united, and the Pacific war became an industrial contest Japan could not win.
- **Lesson:** Surprise against strategic logic; what is targeted matters more than what is destroyed.
- **Debates:** Conspiracy theories (unsupported); why a third strike was not launched.
- **Read:** Naval History and Heritage Command; National Park Service (Pearl Harbor National Memorial); National WWII Museum.

### Malaya and Singapore — 8 December 1941 to 15 February 1942

`singapore-1942` · Campaign · Japanese offensive of 1941–1942 · `Japan|British Empire` · Tomoyuki Yamashita _(new)_; Arthur Percival _(new)_ · Topic: _Speed & moral collapse_

- **Situation:** Singapore, the British naval base, depended on a fleet that never came and on defending Malaya from the north.
- **Plans:** Japanese troops landed in southern Thailand and northern Malaya and advanced fast down the peninsula, outflanking positions by jungle tracks and small coastal landings.
- **Decisive action:** Japanese aircraft sank _Prince of Wales_ and _Repulse_ on 10 December. Japanese forces crossed to Singapore island on 8 February, took the reservoirs and supply dumps, and Percival surrendered.
- **Outcome:** The largest British capitulation in history, followed by the brutal treatment of prisoners and the Sook Ching massacres of Chinese civilians.
- **Lesson:** Tempo and initiative; air power against unprotected ships; the strategic illusion of fortress defence.
- **Debates:** Responsibility for unprepared defences; the "guns pointed the wrong way" myth.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; National Archives of Singapore; Australian War Memorial.

### Coral Sea — 4–8 May 1942

`coral-sea` · Battle · South Pacific operations of 1942 · `United States & Australia|Japan` · Frank Jack Fletcher _(new)_; Shigeyoshi Inoue _(new)_ · Topic: _Carrier warfare & strategic result_

- **Situation:** Japan planned to take Port Moresby by sea. US code-breakers learned of the plan.
- **Decisive action:** In the first battle in which carrier fleets never saw each other, scouting errors on both sides led to strikes on the wrong targets. The light carrier _Shōhō_ was sunk, then _Lexington_. _Shōkaku_ was damaged and _Zuikaku_'s air group depleted.
- **Outcome:** The invasion turned back. Neither large Japanese carrier was available for Midway a month later.
- **Lesson:** A tactical draw can be a strategic victory; reconnaissance under uncertainty.
- **Read:** Naval History and Heritage Command; Australian War Memorial.

### Guadalcanal — 7 August 1942 to 9 February 1943

`guadalcanal` · Campaign · Solomon Islands campaign · `United States & allies|Japan` · Alexander Vandegrift _(new)_; William Halsey _(new)_; Chester Nimitz · Topic: _Attrition & logistics at sea_

- **Situation:** Japan was building an airfield on Guadalcanal that threatened the route to Australia.
- **Plans:** US Marines landed and seized the airfield (Henderson Field). Japan tried to retake it by reinforcing at night with destroyers, the "Tokyo Express".
- **Decisive action:** A string of land and sea battles: the Allied naval disaster at Savo Island, Bloody Ridge, the Santa Cruz carrier battle, and the Naval Battle of Guadalcanal in November. Japan lost aircraft, ships, experienced pilots and starving soldiers faster than it could replace them.
- **Outcome:** Japan evacuated in February 1943, the turning point of the South Pacific.
- **Lesson:** Air cover and logistics for an island base; attrition as a strategy for the stronger industrial power.
- **Read:** Naval History and Heritage Command; National Museum of the Marine Corps; National WWII Museum.

### Battle of the Atlantic — 1939–1945 (focus spring 1943)

`battle-of-the-atlantic` · Campaign · Battle of the Atlantic · `Allied navies & air forces|German U-boat arm` · Karl Dönitz _(new)_; Max Horton _(new)_ · Topic: _Intelligence & technology_

- **Situation:** Britain's survival and any invasion of Europe depended on convoys across the Atlantic.
- **Plans:** Dönitz's "wolf packs" concentrated against convoys. The Allies answered with escorts, air cover and code-breaking.
- **Decisive action:** In spring 1943, very-long-range aircraft closed the mid-Atlantic air gap. Escort carriers, support groups, centimetric radar, HF/DF direction-finding and renewed Ultra decryption all took effect. In May 1943 U-boat losses became unsustainable and Dönitz withdrew from the North Atlantic.
- **Outcome:** Supplies and troops flowed to Britain for the 1944 invasion.
- **Lesson:** Technological and intelligence competition; operational research; measuring success in ships delivered, not U-boats sunk.
- **Debates:** The relative weight of Ultra against technology and industry.
- **Read:** National Museum of the Royal Navy; Imperial War Museums; Naval History and Heritage Command.

### Imphal and Kohima — March to July 1944

`imphal-kohima` · Battle · Burma campaign · `British Empire (Fourteenth Army)|Japan & Indian National Army` · William Slim _(new)_; Renya Mutaguchi _(new)_ · Topic: _Logistics gamble & air supply_

- **Situation:** Mutaguchi's U-Go offensive aimed to take Imphal and its supplies before Allied counteroffensives.
- **Plans:** Japanese troops carried limited supplies, counting on capturing British stocks. Slim planned to fight a defensive battle on the Imphal plain.
- **Decisive action:** Kohima's garrison held. Encircled forces were supplied by air. The Japanese army starved and suffered disease as the monsoon arrived, without breaking through.
- **Outcome:** The worst Japanese land defeat to that date, opening the reconquest of Burma.
- **Lesson:** Logistics as a decisive weapon; air supply; Slim's command methods.
- **Read:** National Army Museum; Imperial War Museums; Kohima Educational Trust.

### Operation Bagration — 22 June to 19 August 1944

`bagration` · Operation · Soviet summer offensive of 1944 · `Soviet Union|Germany` · Konstantin Rokossovsky; Aleksandr Vasilevsky _(new)_; Ivan Chernyakhovsky _(new)_; Ernst Busch _(new)_ · Topic: _Deep operations & deception_

- **Situation:** German forces expected the Soviet summer blow in Ukraine and had moved reserves south. Army Group Centre held a bulge in Belarus.
- **Plans:** Soviet deception (maskirovka) concealed the concentration. Fronts were to break through at several points and drive deep encirclements.
- **Decisive action:** Breakthroughs around Vitebsk and Bobruisk, and Hitler's orders to hold "fortified places", led to the encirclement of large German forces east of Minsk.
- **Outcome:** Army Group Centre was destroyed and the front moved to the Vistula and East Prussia, just as the western Allies fought in Normandy.
- **Lesson:** Deep operations theory in practice; deception; how rigid holding orders aid the attacker.
- **Read:** National WWII Museum; Imperial War Museums.

### Operation Market Garden — 17–25 September 1944

`market-garden` · Operation · North-west Europe campaign (existing) · `Western Allies|Germany` · Bernard Montgomery; Roy Urquhart _(new)_; Walter Model _(new)_ · Topic: _Single-axis risk_

- **Situation:** After the pursuit through France, Montgomery sought a single thrust across the Rhine at Arnhem.
- **Plans:** Three airborne divisions were to seize bridges along one road while XXX Corps drove north to link up.
- **Decisive action:** Drop zones lay far from the Arnhem bridge, radios failed, and II SS Panzer Corps was refitting nearby. Only one battalion (Frost's) reached the bridge. XXX Corps was delayed at Son and Nijmegen on the single road, and the bridge was lost.
- **Outcome:** Most of the 1st Airborne Division was lost; a salient was left toward the Rhine.
- **Lesson:** Planning on optimistic assumptions; ignoring intelligence; a single-road plan has no slack.
- **Debates:** How the intelligence warnings were treated; responsibility among commanders.
- **Read:** National Army Museum; Imperial War Museums; Airborne Museum Hartenstein.

### Leyte Gulf — 23–26 October 1944

`leyte-gulf` · Battle · Philippines campaign of 1944–1945 · `United States & Australia|Japan` · William Halsey; Thomas Kinkaid _(new)_; Takeo Kurita _(new)_; Jisaburō Ozawa _(new)_; Douglas MacArthur · Topic: _Decoys & divided command_

- **Situation:** US forces landed on Leyte. Japan committed its remaining fleet in the Shō-Gō plan.
- **Plans:** Ozawa's carriers, with few aircraft, served as decoys in the north. Kurita's Centre Force and Nishimura's/Shima's force were to converge on the landing area.
- **Decisive action:** Halsey took the bait and ran north with all of Third Fleet. At Surigao Strait, Oldendorf's battleships destroyed the southern force. Off Samar, Kurita's battleships met Taffy 3's escort carriers and destroyers, which fought fiercely. Kurita turned back without reaching the transports. Organised kamikaze attacks began.
- **Outcome:** The Imperial Japanese Navy ceased to be an effective fleet.
- **Lesson:** Unity of command (Halsey under Nimitz, Kinkaid under MacArthur); ambiguous signals; the friction that saved the landing.
- **Debates:** Halsey's decision; Kurita's reasons for withdrawing.
- **Read:** Naval History and Heritage Command; National WWII Museum.

### Battle of the Bulge — 16 December 1944 to 25 January 1945

`battle-of-the-bulge` · Battle · North-west Europe campaign (existing) · `United States & Britain|Germany` · Dwight Eisenhower; George S. Patton; Montgomery; Model; Rundstedt · Topic: _Surprise & reaction_

- **Situation:** Hitler gambled on a winter offensive through the Ardennes to take Antwerp and split the Allies.
- **Plans:** Surprise, bad weather grounding Allied aircraft, and capturing fuel along the way.
- **Decisive action:** Allied intelligence missed the build-up because of radio silence and complacency. Delays at St Vith and the defence of Bastogne blocked the road network. Patton turned Third Army north and relieved Bastogne on 26 December. Clearing weather brought Allied air power back.
- **Outcome:** German armoured reserves were used up. The Malmedy massacre of US prisoners took place.
- **Lesson:** Intelligence complacency; holding road junctions; operational agility (Patton's turn) and command decisions under pressure (Eisenhower's reserves).
- **Debates:** Montgomery's role and his press conference; the "bulge" as Hitler's strategic delusion.
- **Read:** US Army Center of Military History; National WWII Museum; Imperial War Museums.

### Iwo Jima — 19 February to 26 March 1945

`iwo-jima` · Battle · Central Pacific drive · `United States|Japan` · Tadamichi Kuribayashi _(new)_; Holland Smith _(new)_; Nimitz · Topic: _Defence in depth_

- **Situation:** The island's airfields were wanted for fighter escorts and emergency landings on the B-29 route to Japan.
- **Plans:** Kuribayashi gave up beach defence and built a tunnel network. He banned costly banzai charges, aiming to make the cost of capture prohibitive.
- **Decisive action:** Naval bombardment did little against buried positions. US Marines fought for weeks through caves and pillboxes. The flag-raising on Mount Suribachi (23 February) became an icon.
- **Outcome:** Almost the whole garrison died; US losses were among the Marines' heaviest.
- **Lesson:** Defence in depth against overwhelming firepower; the cost calculus of an objective.
- **Debates:** Whether the island was worth its cost.
- **Read:** National Museum of the Marine Corps; Naval History and Heritage Command; National WWII Museum.

### Okinawa — 1 April to 22 June 1945

`okinawa` · Battle · Central Pacific drive · `United States & Britain|Japan` · Simon Bolivar Buckner Jr. _(new)_; Mitsuru Ushijima _(new)_ · Topic: _Attrition & civilian catastrophe_

- **Situation:** Okinawa was the base needed for an invasion of Japan.
- **Plans:** Ushijima let the landings come ashore unopposed and fought from the Shuri Line in the south. Mass kamikaze attacks (the Kikusui operations) targeted the fleet.
- **Decisive action:** Months of attrition broke the Shuri Line. The battleship _Yamato_ was sunk on a one-way sortie. Buckner was killed near the end.
- **Outcome:** A huge share of the Okinawan civilian population died, including in coerced mass suicides. The battle shaped US expectations of an invasion of Japan.
- **Lesson:** Defence in depth; an attacker's attrition at sea; the place of civilians in a battle zone.
- **Read:** National WWII Museum; Naval History and Heritage Command; Okinawa Prefectural Peace Memorial Museum.

### Battle of Berlin — 16 April to 2 May 1945

`berlin-1945` · Operation · Final Soviet offensives of 1945 · `Soviet Union & Polish forces|Germany` · Zhukov; Ivan Konev _(new)_; Rokossovsky; Vasily Chuikov _(new)_; Gotthard Heinrici _(new)_ · Topic: _Rivalry & urban warfare_

- **Situation:** The Red Army stood on the Oder; Stalin wanted Berlin before the western Allies.
- **Plans:** Zhukov's front attacked frontally at the Seelow Heights while Konev's front attacked further south. Stalin encouraged competition between the two marshals.
- **Decisive action:** Heinrici's defence at Seelow held for days. Konev's tanks turned north and the city was encircled. Street fighting reached the Reichstag. Hitler killed himself on 30 April, and the garrison surrendered on 2 May.
- **Outcome:** Germany capitulated within a week. Mass violence against civilians, including rape, accompanied the conquest.
- **Lesson:** Political goals shaping operations; the cost of urban assault; competition between commanders.
- **Read:** Imperial War Museums; National WWII Museum; Deutsch-Russisches Museum Berlin-Karlshorst.
