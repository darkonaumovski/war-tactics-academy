# Leadership Lessons from William Slim: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 24 November 2026, for Volume 9 under `docs/LEADERSHIP_SLIM_PROMPT.md`, after its studies phase (Gallabat, the retreat from Burma, the first Arakan offensive, the Admin Box, Operation Thursday, Meiktila and Mandalay, and the race for Rangoon, `3324e55`). It grades whether each planned lesson can be carried by sources actually opened, and traces every saying in the prompt's traps table. No lesson text exists yet, so nothing here is cited on the site; each batch re-reads what it cites and records it in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

**How the reading was done.** The official histories were read in the Internet Archive's full-text files and searched for the passages named; despatches and Japanese Monographs were read as text converted from their PDFs; web pages were read in the built-in browser. The Phase S reading is listed in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md` and not repeated here. Read for this pass:

- **Slim's own words, openly readable.** "Leadership in Management", his William Queale Lecture of April 1957, printed in the _Australian Army Journal_ in November 1957 and reprinted in an edited version by the Australian Army Research Centre (`https://researchcentre.army.gov.au/library/australian-army-journal-aaj/volume-1-number-1/retrospective-leadership-management`).
- **Biography.** M. D. de B. Collins Persse, "Slim, Sir William Joseph (1891–1970)", _Australian Dictionary of Biography_, vol. 16 (2002), online (`https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/slim-william-joseph-11713`); the Gurkha Museum's online exhibition "'Uncle Bill': Field Marshal Bill Slim and his leadership legacy" and its page "Leadership Style and Legacy" (`https://thegurkhamuseum.co.uk/uncle-bill/`, `https://thegurkhamuseum.co.uk/blog/leadership-and-legacy/`); the National Army Museum's "William Slim: The soldiers' soldier", reread.
- **Official history, further chapters.** Kirby, _The War Against Japan_, vol. III, ch. III ("The Indian Army Prepares for 1943–1944", on Auchinleck's training, health and morale measures and the Bengal famine), ch. XIII and XV–XVI (the prelude to Imphal, the withdrawal of the 17th Division and Kohima), ch. XXIII–XXIV (Sato’s requests and withdrawal) and ch. XXXII (the retrospect on Special Force and Wingate); vol. V, ch. III (Leese's proposal of 7 May 1945 to replace Slim).
- **The Japanese side.** Japanese Monograph No. 134 on Sato's withdrawal from Kohima; Arakawa Kenichi, "Japanese War Leadership in Burma Theater: Imphal", _International Forum on War History 2002: Summary of Sessions_ (National Institute for Defense Studies), already cited by `imphal-kohima`.
- **The record and its words.** "Forgotten Army", _Greymouth Evening Star_, 8 November 1944, a London report of a Ministry of Information press conference, on Papers Past; Rob Kirk, "The Kohima Epitaph: When you go home...", Western Front Association (February 2025), drawing on James Bruce's article for GCHQ; Giffard's despatch on morale (para. 166).
- **The Americans.** Romanus and Sunderland, _Stilwell's Command Problems_, ch. V, for Stilwell's own draft "I am serving under Slim" and the Japanese names for the box tactics.
- **Psychology, abstracts only.** Steven F. Maier and Martin E. P. Seligman, "Learned helplessness at fifty: Insights from neuroscience", _Psychological Review_ 123 (2016), abstract read through Europe PMC (the PMC page is behind a browser check that was not attempted); Laurel W. Oliver, Joan Harman, Elizabeth Hoover, Stephanie M. Hayes and Nancy A. Pandhi, "A quantitative integration of the military cohesion literature", _Military Psychology_ 11 (1999), abstract read on Zenodo.

Identified but not read: _Defeat into Victory_ and _Unofficial History_ (in copyright; the ADB entry and the Gurkha Museum quote both); George MacDonald Fraser's _Quartered Safe Out Here_ (in copyright; the Gurkha Museum quotes a passage); Slim's "Higher Command in War" (1952); any source on the Indian, African or Burmese soldiers' own reasons for serving; any Burmese account of the rising of 1945; Mountbatten's own text for his judgement of Slim; Leese's side of the attempt to replace Slim; the Indian histories of the retreat and the reconquest (not transcribed; lending-restricted); every modern biography (Lewin, Lyman, Hickey and others), none openly readable.

Grades: **strong** means the lesson's main claims and its counter-evidence are in hand. **Adequate** means the argument can be made but named gaps remain. **Thin** means the lesson must be redrawn, merged or dropped unless the gap is filled.

## Shape

- **Route and type.** No type change is needed: a single-commander volume uses `commanderSlug` alone, as Wellington's and Caesar's do. Proposed: `commanderSlug: "william-slim"`, giving `/leadership/william-slim/` and the progress key `leadership/william-slim`. Title: "Leadership Lessons from William Slim: from Gallabat to Rangoon, 1940–1945". Parts: Orientation; Learning to lose; Rebuilding; The decisive battle; Victory.
- **Question**, unchanged: How do you rebuild an army that has learned to lose: by leadership, by means, through the enemy's mistakes, or does the record decide what we see? The pass found the evidence that tests its premise from three sides. Kirby credits the training, health and morale measures of late 1943 to Auchinleck as Commander-in-Chief in India, who set out to restore the morale of an army "badly shaken" by the Arakan failures and to "kill the myth of Japanese invincibility", while Slim was taking command of the new Fourteenth Army; Arakawa, for the Japanese side, says the British and Indian army changed during 1943 and that Mutaguchi took no interest in the change; and the ADB calls the transformation of a defeated force into a proud army Slim's greatest achievement. Leadership, means and the enemy's mistakes all have a witness; the lessons must weigh them, not choose.
- **Fourteen lessons**, as planned. Lesson 6 is adequate only if the soldiers' side is read (correction 9); lessons 1, 3, 10 and 12 are adequate with named gaps.
- **Eight studies** are available to the lessons: the seven of Phase S with `imphal-kohima`.

| "The Forgotten Army"                                         | Origin not traced. The earliest use read is a London report of 7 November 1944: at a Ministry of Information press conference General Owen called the Fourteenth Army the "Forgotten Army", and Air Chief Marshal Joubert spoke of the troops in Burma as if they were all white men, angry that their fighting was not publicised at home. Giffard's despatch of June 1945 says the men were beginning to feel they did not belong to "The Forgotten Army". Kirby credits Wingate with putting "an almost forgotten army" in the headlines. Present as a phrase of the press and the soldiers, with these dates |
| Mountbatten: the finest general of the war                   | Traced to secondary pages only. The National Army Museum gives "The finest general World War Two produced" with Mountbatten's name and the year 1946; the ADB gives the same words without a date. Mountbatten's own text was not found. Use only as these sources report it                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| "Uncle Bill"                                                 | Traced. The ADB says his soldiers came to know him as "Uncle Bill"; the Gurkha Museum says they used the name throughout his senior career. Neither dates its first use                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| Slim's account of morale (spiritual, intellectual, material) | Not found in any openly readable page. His 1957 lecture sets out his view that leadership "is of the spirit" and management "of the mind", and that a leader must be known as an actual person to all under him. Use the lecture, named and dated, and do not reconstruct the memoir's framework                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Slim blaming himself after Imphal's opening                  | The official history says it for him: Kirby writes that the proviso for withdrawing the 17th Division only when a major offensive was certain "was to lead to" the withdrawal being "left until too late", and calls the hurried withdrawal of the 161st Brigade from Kohima, which Slim upheld when asked, "a mistake". The memoir's own admission is not read                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Slim blaming himself at Gallabat                             | Traced to his own words, quoted by the ADB: when two courses were open he had not chosen the bolder, and had "taken counsel of my fears". The ADB does not name the book; it is most likely _Unofficial History_ (1959), and the lesson must say it is a later recollection                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| The Kohima Epitaph                                           | Traced. John Maxwell Edmonds wrote it as an epitaph "For a British Graveyard in France" in the First World War; it was published among his epitaphs in 1918–1920 and its wording was later changed, and it was then used on the 2nd Division's memorial at Kohima. Ban it without its author                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| "Chalo Delhi" and the "March on Delhi"                       | Partly traced. | Mutaguchi's ambition                                         | Traced to an interpretation. | Sato's withdrawal from Kohima                                | Traced, with two framings. Give both                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| The Admin Box as the first defeat of a major Japanese attack | Traced. ## Corrections to the plan

1. **The Indian histories of 1942–1945 are not readable** (corrected in Phase S); the plan's references to them in the curriculum are replaced by Kirby, Madan and the despatches.
2. **The rebuilding began before Slim took the army.** Kirby III ch. III credits Auchinleck with the training, intelligence, patrolling, welfare, health and malaria measures of September–November 1943. Lesson 4 must give Auchinleck's part and Slim's together, and the premise test in the question rests on it.
3. **"Learned helplessness" is out,** except as the reason a label does not fit (Maier and Seligman 2016).
4. **The Kohima Epitaph is Edmonds's,** written for the dead of 1914–1918. Lesson 0 may use it as a case of how memorials become sources, with its author; `lib/validation.ts` rejects it without his name.
5. **"Leadership is just plain you" is untraced** and banned; Slim's own 1957 lecture is the openly readable statement of his views and makes the management-book risk of rule 12 concrete, since he himself addressed managers.
6. **Joubert's "angry white men"** belongs in lessons 0 and 6: the press conference that named the "Forgotten Army" described an army that was largely Indian and African as white. Give no figures from it.
7. **Gallabat's self-blame is a later recollection,** quoted through the ADB from Slim's own book; lesson 1 must date it as such and set it beside the official accounts of the tanks and the air.
8. **Irwin's attempt to relieve Slim** is in the ADB but not in Kirby; lesson 3 should say so and quote Irwin's reported message only as the ADB reports it.
9. **Lesson 6 needs the soldiers' side.** Nothing read gives the Indian, African, Gurkha or Burmese soldiers' own reasons. Recommendation: read an openly readable museum or archive source on the Indian Army's wartime expansion and on the African divisions before Part II; if none can carry it, fold lesson 6 into lessons 4 and 13 and keep the famine and the Indian National Army there.
10. **Slim's memoir reaches the volume only through others,** chiefly the ADB, the Gurkha Museum and Kirby's quotations of his contemporary appreciations; each lesson must say which.
11. **Counter-cases proposed** (catalog profiles and studies):
    - Lesson 1: Oskar Potiorek (`oskar-potiorek`, `cer`, `drina-1914`, `kolubara`), whose first defeat in Serbia was followed by a second invasion on the same lines.
    - Lesson 2: link the Serbian volume's `the-retreat-that-kept-an-army`; Napoleon's retreat from Moscow in prose for an army that did not survive its retreat.
    - Lesson 3: James Longstreet at Gettysburg (`james-longstreet`, `gettysburg`), a senior subordinate whose advice was overruled; link the Western Front's `what-reaches-the-top`.
    - Lesson 4: Robert E. Lee's army in the winter of 1864–1865 (`robert-e-lee`, `petersburg`), whose morale could not survive without the means; link Wellington's `the-scum-of-the-earth` and Caesar's `my-soldiers`.
    - Lesson 5: the German Sixth Army at Stalingrad (`stalingrad`, with `erich-von-manstein`), told to stand and promised an airlift that could not supply it.
    - Lesson 6: link the Hannibal volume's `an-army-of-many-peoples`; a counter-case to be proposed in its batch.
    - Lesson 7: link Grant's `surprised-at-shiloh`; Mutaguchi's own plan as the mirror.
    - Lesson 8: Isoroku Yamamoto at Midway (`isoroku-yamamoto`, `midway`), whose gamble was read by the enemy; link the Hannibal volume's `reading-the-consul`.
    - Lesson 9: link Wellington's `a-short-leash` and Caesar's `lieutenants`.
    - Lesson 10: Erwin Rommel at the end of the Axis line in North Africa (`erwin-rommel`, `el-alamein`); link Wellington's `allies-he-could-not-command` and the Western Front's `coordination-by-persuasion`.
    - Lesson 11: Helmuth von Moltke the Younger in 1914 (`helmuth-von-moltke-the-younger`, `marne-1914`), whose plan was not changed in time; link Napoleon's `plan-for-being-wrong`.
    - Lesson 12: link the Hannibal volume's `both-rejected`, for Scipio's treatment after victory.
    - Lesson 13: the Serbian volume's `the-ledger-of-survival` or the Western Front's `the-ledger-of-learning`, as planned.
12. **Rule 4 extended.** The despatches, the official histories and the 1944 press report give casualty and strength figures throughout; none is to be carried into a lesson, including the Japanese losses at Imphal and in the Sittang breakout.
