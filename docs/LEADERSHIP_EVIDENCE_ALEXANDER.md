# Leadership Lessons from Alexander the Great: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 10 November 2026, for Volume 2 under `docs/LEADERSHIP_NEXT_VOLUMES_PROMPT.md`. It grades whether each planned lesson can be carried by sources that were actually opened in this session. No lesson text exists yet, so nothing here is cited on the site; each batch re-reads what it cites and records it in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

**How the reading was done.** Arrian (Chinnock, 1884, Wikisource), Plutarch's _Alexander_ (Perrin, Loeb 1919, LacusCurtius) and Diodorus 17 (Loeb vol. VIII, 1963, LacusCurtius) were read chapter by chapter for the passages each lesson needs, not from cover to cover. Livius.org and Wikisource pages were read through a page-reading tool that returns a summary, so exact wording must be re-read before a lesson quotes it. Encyclopaedia Iranica refuses scripted requests and was read in full in the built-in browser. Livy was downloaded from Project Gutenberg and searched. Psychology papers were read as abstracts only, through OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or Crossref records for the cited DOI. The LacusCurtius pages for Plutarch and Diodorus state that their texts are in the public domain; Chinnock's translation dates from 1884.

Grades: **strong** means the lesson's main claims and its counter-evidence are in hand. **Adequate** means the argument can be made but named gaps remain. **Thin** means the lesson must be redrawn, merged or dropped.

## Corrections to the plan

1. **"To the strongest" is a translation choice.** Chinnock's Arrian (7.26) has the dying king answer "To the best", introduced as what "some authors" relate. Diodorus 17.117 has "To the strongest", and the Loeb note says the Greek word can mean "best" as well as "most powerful", and that Diodorus's book 18 gives "To the best". Lesson 12 should present the answer as reported, with both renderings and the hedge.
2. **The case for Darius is Badian's.** The prompt credits Briant. What was read is Badian's Iranica article on Darius III (1994): the Greek historians charged him with cowardice, though bravery was his best-attested quality, and his flight at Issus probably kept the only person who could hold the empire together alive. Briant's _Darius in the Shadow of Alexander_ was read only through Llewellyn-Jones's review (2015), which says Darius is "invariably placed in Alexander's shadow". The Gaugamela diary adds that "the king, his troops deserted him", which the Livius note says has embarrassed classicists.
3. **Parmenion is not always wrong in the record.** Arrian, the source most often blamed for the pattern, says at Persepolis that Alexander did not act with prudence when he rejected Parmenion's advice (3.18). Plutarch reports a "general complaint" that Parmenion was sluggish at Gaugamela, quoting only the words "arrogance and pomp" from Callisthenes (33). The "if I were Alexander" exchange and the night-attack advice are both introduced in Arrian with "it is said". Lesson 5 is stronger for this: the pattern exists, but not uniformly, and Badian warns that the documents quoted in the Greek sources are "as usual fictitious".
4. **The romance of leadership is contested.** Meindl, Ehrlich and Dukerich (1985) could not be read; Schyns and Bligh (2007) describe the idea in their abstract. Hammond, Schyns, Lester and Clapp-Smith (2021) report four replications of Meindl and Ehrlich (1987) that do not support the original finding. Use `base: "contested"` in the orientation lesson, as the Napoleon volume did for escalation of commitment.
5. **The Opis prayer, as Chinnock renders it,** asks for "harmony and community of rule" between Macedonians and Persians (7.11), not "concord" or "partnership". Tarn's reading of it as a declaration of human unity, and Badian's rejection in 1958, are known here only from the abstract of Majidi and Zarrinkoob (2021). Neither original was read.
6. **Polybius 11.19** says Hannibal kept his army "free from sedition" towards him or among themselves for sixteen years. The Loeb page notes that the text is not yet proofread.
7. **Gedrosia as punishment has no support in what was read.** Arrian cites Nearchus alone for the motive: the wish to outdo Semiramis and Cyrus, whose armies were said to have barely survived the desert, and to bring the army nearer the fleet (6.24). Briant describes Nearchus's voyage as a supply mission for the land army. Leave the punishment theory out unless a modern source that argues it is read.
8. **Massaga is the editors' identification.** Plutarch does not name the town where he says Alexander attacked departing mercenaries under truce; he calls the act a stain on his career (59). The Loeb notes to Plutarch and to Diodorus 17.84 place it at Massaga.
9. **Plutarch puts the refusal at the Ganges** (62); Arrian and Diodorus at the Hyphasis. Plutarch has the soldiers wailing at the tent until Alexander relents; Arrian has three days of seclusion and unfavourable sacrifices, on Ptolemy's authority. A good source check for lesson 6.
10. **The "Persian youths" differ by source.** Plutarch 47 speaks of thirty thousand boys taught Greek and Macedonian weapons, without saying they were Persian. Diodorus calls them thirty thousand Persians, formed as a counterweight to the phalanx. Briant cites Curtius for a levy of thirty thousand youths serving as soldiers and hostages. Arrian's account of the Epigoni (7.6) was not read.
11. **The Granicus disagreement is real.** Plutarch 16 agrees with Arrian that Parmenion objected and Alexander crossed at once; Diodorus 17.19 has the army cross at dawn unopposed. The orientation lesson can use it as planned.
12. **The water poured out in the desert** is placed in Gedrosia or among the Parapamisadae by Arrian (6.26), during the pursuit of Darius by Plutarch (42), and elsewhere by others, as Chinnock's note records. It suits lesson 2 or 11 as a story whose place the sources cannot agree on.
13. **The cross-volume themes field already exists.** `LeadershipLesson` already has `themes: LeadershipTheme[]` with eight values. The proposed themes page should build on that field, not add another.
