# Leadership Lessons from Hannibal and Scipio: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 20 November 2026, for Volume 7 under `docs/LEADERSHIP_HANNIBAL_SCIPIO_PROMPT.md`, after its studies phase (the Ticinus, the Trebia, Lake Trasimene, New Carthage, Baecula, the Metaurus and Ilipa, `28a8bca`). - **Question**, unchanged: Why did the better tactician lose the war: Hannibal's strategy, Rome's system, Scipio's command, or the record written by the winners? The pass found the two ancient verdicts that frame it. Polybius admires Hannibal for holding an army of many peoples together for sixteen years, then says that had he begun with the rest of the world and finished with the Romans none of his plans would have failed, but he began with those he should have left to last (11.19). The same Polybius ends the book of Cannae by crediting Rome's recovery to the peculiar virtues of its constitution and to wise counsel (3.118). The orientation states both as claims to test.
- **Thirteen lessons**, as planned, with one slug changed (correction 24). None is thin; lesson 4 is adequate and must build on `opis` without repeating it.

## Corrections to the plan

1. **The oath is a promise never to be Rome's friend**, in Polybius, sworn at nine and told by Hannibal to Antiochus; Livy's version, enmity as soon as he could, is "a story widely current". The orientation keeps both chains.
2. **The motto "find a way or make one" was not found**; it is banned.
3. **The vinegar is Livy's alone**, and Polybius's account of the same place has the soldiers building up a broken path. Polybius's claim to have crossed the Alps himself (3.48) is the right setting for the test.
4. **Maharbal's saying is Livy's**, with Livy's own view that the delay saved Rome; the Alexander volume has it, so lesson 1 links rather than repeats.
5. **"Hannibal at the gates" is both an event and a phrase.** Livy 26.10 has him ride to the Colline Gate in 211; the proverb in Latin is Cicero's hypothetical and Livy's literal phrase at Nola. The prompt's view that it was not a scene of the war is half right: the ride was, the proverb was not.
6. **The Ennius verse is quoted by Livy and by Cicero**, so it can be used with both reporters.
7. **The continence story has two incompatible endings** (Polybius: her father; Livy: her betrothed). Lesson 8 gives both.
8. **The tide has two hours** (Polybius's usual evening ebb; Livy's midday with a north wind), already recorded in the New Carthage study.
9. **Livy does not know who asked for the meeting at Zama** and records Valerius Antias's different version; Polybius gives Hannibal the initiative after the spies.
10. **The Ephesus ranking comes from Acilius through Claudius**, in a passage where Livy's own narrative has a different Roman, Villius, talking to Hannibal.
11. **Hannibal's last words are Livy's composition**, and Plutarch says there were several accounts of his death.
12. **"Carthage must be destroyed" is "it is said" in Plutarch**, in Greek, fifty years after the war; banned in Latin.
13. **The camps were burned under cover of negotiations, not a truce.** Polybius 14.1–2: Scipio let Syphax believe a peace was possible while his envoys studied the huts. Lesson 8's shadow says so.
14. **No source read says Scipio learned his methods from Hannibal.** Polybius says Scipio studied the causes of his father's defeat (10.7) and, at Ilipa, reversed his own pattern (11.22). The volume's claim of a method passing from one side to the other is an inference, and lesson 7 presents it as one, with the Trebia and Ilipa side by side.
15. **Carthage did vote reinforcements after Cannae** (Livy 23.13), over Hanno's objection that Rome had not mentioned peace. The phrase "without a state that could reinforce it" in the prompt needs qualifying; what reached Hannibal is a separate question the batches must source.
16. **Even the Latin colonies wavered**: twelve of thirty refused men and money in 209, complaining that Hannibal sent their captured men home without ransom while Rome sent its own to Sicily (Livy 27.9). Lesson 3 uses it as the cost of loyalty, not only its proof.
17. **Rome thanked its defeated consul after Cannae** (Livy 22.61), and Livy says a Carthaginian general would have been tortured. Lessons 5 and 11 can use the contrast, with Livy's patriotism named.
18. **Livy's portrait of Hannibal is a patriotic set piece** (21.4): every soldier's virtue, and every vice Rome attributed to Carthage. The orientation uses it as a test of Livy.
19. **Scipio's religion has three readings**: Polybius, policy (10.2); Livy, open (26.19); Appian, belief that grew on him (_Spanish Wars_ 23). Lesson 6 gives all three.
20. **Scipio's last years cannot be told with confidence.** Livy cannot decide who prosecuted him, when he died or where he was buried (38.56), and disputes Polybius's date (39.52); Polybius tells his defiance "for the sake of the good fame of the departed" (23.14).
21. **Scipio defended Hannibal at Rome** (Livy 33.47) and Plutarch contrasts his magnanimity with Flamininus's pursuit (_Flamininus_ 21). Lesson 11 may use both.
22. **Polybius's two verdicts frame the question** (11.19 on strategy; 3.118 on Rome's constitution) and are added to the orientation and the ledger.
23. **Hannibal's historians reach us only through others.** Sosylus is named by Polybius as gossip (3.20), Silenus by Livy for a figure he doubts (26.49); Nepos, who names both, was not read. The orientation says so.
24. **Lesson 10 is renamed `the-meeting-at-zama`**, so that its slug does not repeat the study's.
25. **Lesson 0's counter-case is Caesar**, whose self-written record (`how-to-read-caesar`) is the opposite of a record written by the enemy's friends.
26. **Phase S findings stand**: the Ticinus rescue through Laelius, the Metaurus fragment, the reversed order at Ilipa, the names Silpia and Carmo, and the dates.
27. **Scipio's age is disputed.** Polybius makes him twenty-seven when he crossed the Ebro (10.6); Livy says barely twenty-four at his election (26.18), as does Appian (_Spanish Wars_ 18). Lesson 6 gives both rather than an age.
28. **Figures throughout.** The ancient numbers for the armies, the Alps, the elephants, Cannae, Trasimene, Zama, the Latin colonies, the ransom and the booty are left out.
29. **The modern reference denies what Polybius asserts.** The 1911 Britannica's "Punic Wars" says Hannibal's failure was not due to any strategic mistake but to Roman will and the compactness of the Italian confederacy, "individual genius overborne by corporate and persevering effort"; Polybius 11.19 says he began with the enemy he should have left to last. The two are the candidate answers "Hannibal's strategy" and "Rome's system" in their sharpest form, and the orientation and the ledger set them side by side.
30. **Polybius's closeness to the Scipios is confirmed** by the 1911 Britannica's "Polybius": the lifelong friend of Scipio Aemilianus, the future conqueror of Carthage, who more than once paid for his researches. The orientation can say so with that source.
31. **Lesson 5's counter-case is Pompey** (added in batch H1), because Barclay already serves the same point in `the-only-army`; lesson 10 needs another counter-case.
32. **Lesson 7's counter-case is Hasdrubal son of Gisco at Ilipa** (added in batch H1), who learned Scipio's routine and was deceived by it.
33. **Lesson 2's counter-case is Marmont at Salamanca** (added in batch H2), who read a pattern of retreat as Wellington's character (Oman V, pp. 421–431). Napoleon misjudging Wellington was not used, because no source read gives the misjudgement.
34. **Lesson 4's counter-case is Napoleon's army of 1812–1813** (added in batch H2), whose allied contingents followed their governments after the retreat from Moscow (Britannica); Wellington's allied army is linked as the opposite case, because it held.
35. **Tarentum and the Italian soldiers were read in batch H2**: Livy 25.7–10 (the hostages scourged and thrown from the Rock, the terms, the betrayal) and 27.15–16 (the recapture); Appian, _War against Hannibal_ 56–61, and Livy 30.20 on the Italians who refused to sail, in two incompatible versions, both given as uncertain.
36. **At Cannae Polybius makes battle the senate's decision** (3.107), not only Varro's; lesson 2 sets this against the portrait of a rash consul, beside Jona Lendering's dissent on Flaminius.
37. **Lesson 6's counter-case is the young Pompey in Spain** (added in batch H3), not Alexander: Plutarch, _Pompey_ 6–8 and 17–20, gives a commander who took command at twenty-three on his own authority and, in Spain against Sertorius, hurried into battle for a victory of his own. Alexander's `a-borrowed-army` is linked in the strategist's reading.
38. **Scipio's religion has a fourth reading**: the 1911 Britannica thinks it quite possible that he honestly shared the belief that he was favoured by heaven, and notes that his memoirs, written in Greek, are lost. Lesson 6 gives it beside Polybius, Livy and Appian.
39. **The revolt of Indibilis and Mandonius was read in batch H3**: Livy 28.24 and 28.34 (the rising on the rumour of Scipio's death, and the second pardon without hostages), Polybius 11.25–33 (the mutiny at Sucro and the battle) and Livy 29.1–3 (the second rising after Scipio left, which Livy puts down to admiration for Scipio alone). Lesson 8 uses it as the limit of a personal generosity.
40. **Lesson 8's counter-case is Caesar's pardoned enemies** (Suetonius 75 and 80; Plutarch, _Caesar_ 57), set beside Indibilis rather than retelling `clemency`; lesson 9's is Caesar at the Rubicon (Suetonius 29–31), set beside the tribunes' ruling of 205.
41. **Lesson 10's counter-case is Darius III at Gaugamela** (added in batch H4), not Pompey, who had already served twice: Arrian 3.8 and 3.13 give a commander who levelled the ground for chariots after losing at Issus, and Alexander's men opening their ranks to let the chariots through, the same answer as Scipio's lanes at Zama.
42. **Lesson 11's counter-case is Zhukov** (added in batch H4), not Napoleon or Wellington: Britannica's biography gives a commander sent to obscure commands by Stalin after 1945 and dismissed by Khrushchev in 1957, months after using the army's aircraft to save him. Livy 38.56's other version of Scipio's last years, in which he freed his brother from arrest by force, is given beside the story of the Capitol.
43. **Hannibal's intervention in the Carthaginian senate after Zama** is "it is said" in Polybius 15.19; Livy 30.37 names the senator Gisgo. Both reporters are given.
44. **Lesson 12's counter-case is Napoleon in 1813** (added in batch H4), whose enemies agreed to avoid him and beat his subordinates (Britannica), set beside Rome's treatment of Hannibal; the Napoleon volume's `your-rivals-are-learning` is linked. The ledger answers the question with Rome's system first, Scipio's command second, Hannibal's strategy third and the record over all three, and invites the opposite argument.

## Psychology to read in the batches

- Behaviour-modelling training (lesson 7): Taylor, Russ-Eft and Chan (2005), a meta-analysis of 117 studies, abstract only. Effects largest on learning, smaller on job behaviour; transfer greatest when negative and positive models are shown together. The limit: designed training with feedback, not learning from an enemy in war; the lesson may say only that learning from others' failures as well as successes is the pattern that transfers.
- Work-group diversity (lesson 4): van Knippenberg and Schippers (2007), a review in the _Annual Review of Psychology_, abstract only. Diversity can help or harm group processes and performance, and the mechanisms and contingencies are unclear. The limit: workplace groups; it supports only the claim that there is no simple rule.
- Correspondence bias (lesson 2): Gilbert and Malone (1995), abstract only, already used; reuse with a link only for the risk of reading a whole character from one act, or `base: "none"`.
- Legitimacy (lesson 9): Tyler (2006), already used twice; `base: "none"` unless the lesson asks a different question.

**Hannibal** (`hannibal-barca`), sourced to Charles Oughton's article for the Society for Classical Studies, which was not re-read in this pass.

- **Source.** The 1911 Britannica's "Hannibal (general)" (read), with Livius's "Hannibal Barca" re-read; Britannica's modern biography if it opens in the browser.

Dates not given in what this pass read are to be taken from the sources in the batch.

**Scipio** (`scipio-africanus`), sourced to a National Gallery glossary entry on Scipio in painting, which is about reception, not his career.

- **Source.** The 1911 Britannica's "Scipio" (read), with Polybius 10.2–3 for his contemporaries' picture of him.
- **Strength and limitation.** Strength: intelligence and preparation (New Carthage), the reversed order at Ilipa, and winning peoples through the hostages; limitation: Locri and Pleminius, and an authority that he stretched in 205 and that turned on him later.
- **Timeline.** Add the Ticinus (218, as a seventeen-year-old in his first campaign, in Polybius's account from Laelius), Baecula (209 or 208), the consulship and the debate on Africa (205), Locri, the burning of the camps and the Great Plains (203), and the prosecution and withdrawal to Liternum (date uncertain, as Livy says).
