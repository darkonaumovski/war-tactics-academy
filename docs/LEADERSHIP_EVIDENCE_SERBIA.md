# Leadership Lessons from the Serbian Supreme Command, 1914–1918: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 11 November 2026, for Volume 3 under `docs/LEADERSHIP_NEXT_VOLUMES_PROMPT.md`. It grades whether each planned lesson can be carried by sources that were actually opened in this session. No lesson text exists yet, so nothing here is cited on the site; each batch re-reads what it cites and records it in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

**How the reading was done.** The 1914-1918-online encyclopedia now puts its article pages behind an automated bot check (Anubis), which this project does not try to pass. The encyclopedia also publishes each article as a PDF at the article address with `?format=pdf`; those files are served openly and were downloaded and read in full as text: Bjelajac, "Serbia"; Tasić, "Warfare 1914-1918 (South East Europe)" and "Pecanac, Kosta"; Hall, "War in the Balkans" and "Bulgaria"; Ristović, "Occupation during and after the War (South East Europe)"; Raspopović, "Montenegro"; Krivokapić-Jović, "Historiography 1918-Today: Serbia and Montenegro". Britannica was read in the built-in browser. The Historical Museum of Serbia and Military Museum catalogue _Serbia 1914_, Reiss's report, the Carnegie report, volume III of the Austro-Hungarian official history and Monica Stanley's diary were downloaded and searched in full text. Ministry of Defence pages were read through a summarising tool. Psychology papers were read as abstracts only.

Grades: **strong** means the lesson's main claims and its counter-evidence are in hand. **Adequate** means the argument can be made but named gaps remain. **Thin** means the lesson must be redrawn, merged or dropped.

## Corrections to the plan

1. **The subordinate who overruled the plan is not in the sources read.** The curriculum has Mišić insisting on withdrawing the First Army and Putnik consenting. The museums' catalogue says the Supreme Command ordered the withdrawal to shorten an over-extended front. The Ministry of Defence page on Kolubara says the counter-offensive was decided "according to the suggestion of General Misic", which credits him with the counter-stroke, not with overruling a withdrawal. Britannica credits Kolubara to Putnik. The overruling story appears in popular accounts that cannot be cited. Lesson 4 should be merged into lesson 3, with the question of who gets the credit as its source check.
2. **Mišić's memoir is not a source for 1914.** A summary seen in search results says it runs to the end of the Second Balkan War; the only copy found online is a third-party upload of a 1990 edited edition, which this project will not cite.
3. **The French shells of December 1914 were unverified in this pass** (resolved in batch S1: Rauchensteiner, p. 278, describes French artillery ammunition brought by the Salonika–Niš railway). Tasić says the withdrawal let the army replenish its ammunition; Bjelajac records Russian rifles, cartridges and some shells sent after September. No source read names French shells arriving before the counter-offensive.
4. **The 1915 epidemic is named differently.** Bjelajac says typhus, Tasić "typhoid fever", Hall "cholera and typhus", the Austro-Hungarian official history "Hungertyphus". Lessons should use the source's own word.
5. **Toplica was not timed by the Supreme Command.** Tasić says Pećanac, flown in from Salonika, had precise instructions to prepare an uprising for the Allied breakthrough, and that other chetnik leaders insisted on immediate action; Bjelajac says the High Command's envoy was to act "not before the beginning of the main assault from the south" and joined "reluctantly". The lesson's claim should become: when a distant command sends agents to people it cannot protect, it may lose control of the timing, and the cost still falls on those people.
6. **The 1,300 corporals rest on commemorative sources.** The two books found are a 1969 history published by the corporals' own veterans' association and a 2014 local-library monograph; neither was read, and no source read gives their number, training or effect. Use them only as an example of how the war is remembered.
7. **The assassination is itself a historiographical dispute.** Bjelajac writes that it was "masterminded and carried out by the local members of Young Bosnia"; Hall says a cabal of officers led by Dimitrijević "abetted" it; Britannica calls the Black Hand "instrumental in planning" it. The orientation lesson can show this as an example of national and international traditions disagreeing.
8. **The Salonika trial's outcome differs inside Britannica.** Its Black Hand article says Dimitrijević "and two others were executed"; a search summary of its Dimitrijević biography gives a different count, and that page was not opened. Use the article that was read, and give no figure.
9. **Bjelajac's verdict on prisoners is a claim.** He writes that Serbia "fully obeyed the Hague and Geneva conventions" and that the prisoners' sufferings were not due to ill will, citing an International Commission that was not read. He is a Serbian historian answering Austro-Hungarian wartime propaganda; the ledger lesson presents his view with that stake.
10. **Stepanović's initiative at Cer is unsupported.** Tasić and Bjelajac credit the Second Army; the Ministry of Defence page credits Stepanović with the victory but says nothing about acting on his own. Lesson 1 should not claim it.
11. **The Allies refused a Serbian initiative too.** In 1915, Tasić says, Serbia proposed a pre-emptive strike on Bulgaria before its mobilisation was complete, and the western Allies strongly opposed it. This belongs in lesson 2 beside Syrmia and Romanija, and in lesson 7.
12. **Putnik's outbreak of war.** Britannica says he was in Austria when war began and was escorted to Romania, and resumed command in poor health. A useful detail for the orientation lesson or lesson 6.
13. **The enemy's file praises the defeated.** The Austro-Hungarian official history calls the Serbian army brave and says it fought "ohne jede Aussicht auf Erfolg" to gain time for the French and British. It is a witness from the other side, and it is quoted in German only in the audit; lessons paraphrase it.
14. **The regent's place.** Britannica says Prince Alexander was appointed regent by the ailing King Peter on 24 June 1914 and served as commander-in-chief throughout the war, and that he brought the Black Hand's leaders to trial "on dubious charges" to remove a rival. His profile (`crown-prince-alexander`) could be added to the volume's `alsoCommanderSlugs`; this is a decision for approval.
