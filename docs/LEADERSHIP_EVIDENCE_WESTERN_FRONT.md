# Leadership Lessons from Foch and Haig, the Western Front, 1914–1918: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 12 November 2026, for Volume 4, which `docs/LEADERSHIP_NEXT_VOLUMES_PROMPT.md` lists among the later volumes ("Did the generals learn, and was the learning worth its price?") without a curriculum. This pass therefore proposes the curriculum as well as grading it. It grades whether each planned lesson can be carried by sources actually opened in this session. No lesson text exists yet, so nothing here is cited on the site; each batch re-reads what it cites and records it in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

**How the reading was done.** The 1914-1918-online encyclopedia, the backbone of the Serbian volume, now puts its PDF editions behind the same automated bot check (Anubis) as its article pages: every `?format=pdf` request, including one for an article read on 11 November, returned the check page. This project does not try to pass it, so no encyclopedia article could be read, and those already cited by the catalog's studies (Philpott on the Somme, the articles on Amiens, Ypres and the spring offensives) cannot be re-read for this volume. The National Library of Scotland's pages on the Haig papers show a human-verification check, which was also not passed. The volume therefore rests on three kinds of source that could be read in full:

- **Primary texts in the public domain**, downloaded from the Internet Archive and read as text: _Sir Douglas Haig's Despatches (December 1915–April 1919)_, edited by J. H. Boraston (Dent, 1919), with Foch's introduction; _The Memoirs of Marshal Foch_, translated by T. Bentley Mott (Heinemann, 1931), Digital Library of India scan (OCR with errors; page numbers legible).
- **Reference and museum pages**, read in the built-in browser where automated fetching is refused: Britannica on Haig, on Foch (Charles-André Laffargue), on the First Battle of the Somme (quoting the British official history at length), on Passchendaele (from _The Canadian Encyclopedia_; R. H. Roy and Richard Foot) and on Amiens (Brereton Greenhous and Jon Tattrie); the National Army Museum on Haig and on the Somme; UNESCO UK on the Haig diary as a Memory of the World inscription.
- **Scholarship and records openly published**: William Philpott, "Beyond the 'Learning Curve'" (RUSI commentary, 10 November 2009); Peter Crook, "Should the Third Battle of Ypres have been called off in October 1917?" (Western Front Association, 2008); the Armed Forces Act 2006, section 359 (legislation.gov.uk); and for psychology, the PubMed abstracts of Keiser and Arthur (2021), a meta-analysis of after-action reviews, and Tannenbaum and Cerasoli (2013), a meta-analysis of debriefs.

Identified but not yet read: Foch's _The Principles of War_ (1918 and 1920 translations on the Internet Archive); Haig's diary in any form (the Sheffield and Bourne edition is in copyright; the manuscript and typescript are at the National Library of Scotland); the British, Australian and Canadian official histories.

Grades: **strong** means the lesson's main claims and its counter-evidence are in hand. **Adequate** means the argument can be made but named gaps remain. **Thin** means the lesson must be redrawn, merged or dropped.

## Proposed shape

- **Subject and route.** A shared volume on two commanders who were for most of the war neither each other's superior nor subordinate. Proposed `commanderSlug: "douglas-haig"` with `alsoCommanderSlugs: ["ferdinand-foch"]`, so the route is `/leadership/douglas-haig/` and both profiles lead to it. The question is about the British "learning curve" debate, which centres on Haig; Foch's place is the coalition. (A decision for approval: the route could follow Foch instead.)
- **Title.** Leadership Lessons from Foch and Haig: the Western Front, 1914–1918.
- **Question.** Did the generals learn, and was the learning worth its price? The second half is the volume's ledger, and Haig gave his own answer in his final despatch: "in it the price of victory is paid", with "no way of avoiding payment of the price". The last lesson tests that answer.
- **Parts.** Orientation; Learning under fire, 1914–1916; The cost of persistence, 1917; Sharing command, 1918; Ending and accounting.
- **Twelve lessons** (below), from a thirteen-lesson draft with the Ypres 1914 lesson merged into lesson 7.

## Corrections to the plan

1. **The despatches are not Haig's unmediated voice.** They were edited for publication in 1919 by J. H. Boraston, his private secretary; the published text marks cuts with rows of asterisks (for example in the account of the defensive preparations of early 1918); and footnotes added in 1919 supply reasons the despatches did not give, such as Major-General Davidson's list, from a Commons speech of 6 August 1919, for continuing Third Ypres. Lessons must cite them as Haig's public account, edited, not as his reasoning at the time.
2. **The Somme's aims differ by source.** The despatch of December 1916 gives the offensive three objects, relieving Verdun, holding German forces in the west and wearing down the enemy, and says all three "had already been achieved". The NAM and Britannica say Haig planned for a breakthrough, with cavalry to ride through to Bapaume. The lesson sets the plan beside the account written after it.
3. **Who proposed Foch at Doullens: the sources differ in emphasis more than in fact.** Foch's memoir says that on 24 March Haig telegraphed the Chief of the Imperial General Staff to come to France so that a Supreme Command could be created, that at Abbeville on 25 March he proposed that Foch be named commander-in-chief, and that at Doullens on 26 March Milner proposed the coordinating role to Clemenceau, after which Haig asked for the wider wording. It also quotes Milner's memorandum that Foch "did not wish to command anything" but wanted the position he had held at Ypres in 1914, with higher authority. Foch's introduction to Haig's despatches credits Haig's interventions of 24 and 26 March. Britannica says Haig "secured the appointment". (Corrected during batch W1: the first draft of this pass read the memoir's Doullens chapter without its account of 24–25 March.)
4. **Haig's diary cannot be read here, and its revision is contested.** Search summaries describe a manuscript and a post-war typescript "with additions and alterations"; one says the Sheffield and Bourne edition found the changes "generally mundane" with two exceptions, and that Denis Winter's charge of rewriting is disputed. None of this was read at first hand, so the volume must not say the diary was doctored, and should cite diary words only as quoted by a source read (as Britannica quotes the eve-of-Somme entry).
5. **Haig had argued against unity of command, in his own words.** The despatch of December 1916 says unity of command "is usually essential" but that on the Somme "the cordial good feeling between the Allied Armies" proved "equally effective". This belongs in lesson 6, beside his request at Doullens.
6. **Two famous sayings are untraced.** Foch's "My centre is giving way, my right is retreating; situation excellent, I attack" is not in his memoir's account of the Marne, and no contemporary record of it was found; the only sources found are secondary and one calls it probably apocryphal. "Tout le monde à la bataille" ("everyone to battle") is not in the memoir either. Neither is used unless a named origin is found; add both to the banned sayings in `lib/validation.ts`.
7. **"Lions led by donkeys" is a later phrase, not a wartime quotation.** The NAM names Alan Clark's _The Donkeys_ (1961) among the attacks on Haig's reputation. The volume uses the phrase only as the title of a later view, with no attribution to a wartime German officer.
8. **Haig's optimism is the recurring evidence, and it has a context.** Britannica records Haig telling the government in October 1917 that German resistance might break "at any moment" and that German losses exceeded British "not improbably by a hundred per cent", which it says was untrue; Haig's despatch cites captured documents showing that German methods were failing. The psychologist's reading of optimism must stay interpretive, and the strategist must show what Haig could and could not know.
9. **The 1918 record reverses the pattern, and the volume should show it.** At Amiens Haig argued against renewing a stalled attack and Foch "definitely came around" (Foch's memoir); Britannica credits Currie with urging consolidation. On armistice terms Haig argued for moderation because the German army was "not so broken" (Foch's memoir). A volume that reads only 1916–17 would miss half the answer to its question.
10. **Foch is also a counter-case in the Serbian volume** (`when-allies-ask-you-to-attack`). Lessons here build on that reading of Foch at the coalition's centre rather than repeat it.
11. **Britannica's Foch article is a witness with a side.** It calls Foch "generally considered the leader most responsible for the Allied victory" and has Haig "thinking about his embarkation ports" on 24 March. Its author writes in the French tradition; the claim about the ports needs a British source before it is used as fact.
12. **No casualty figures.** Britannica gives figures for every battle in this volume, and they differ between articles. Lessons describe losses without numbers, as in the earlier volumes.
13. **The pardons of 2006 did not quash the convictions.** Section 359 of the Armed Forces Act 2006 pardons those executed for disciplinary offences in the First World War but "does not affect any conviction or sentence". The ledger lesson uses that wording.

## Psychology to read in the batches

- After-action reviews and debriefs (lessons 5 and 8): Keiser and Arthur (2021), 61 studies, and Tannenbaum and Cerasoli (2013), 46 samples, both find that structured reviews improve later performance. Both study training and work settings, not armies at war, and say nothing about an organisation of a million people learning under fire; the limit has to say so.
- Voice and silence (lessons 2 and 4): Detert and Burris (2007), already cited by the Napoleon volume.
- Escalation of commitment (lessons 3 and 8): the research cited by the Napoleon volume, re-read in the batch.
- Confirmation bias in assessment (lesson 4): a review abstract to be read in the batch.
- Lessons 1, 7, 9, 10 and 11 plan `base: "none"` unless a sound source is found.
