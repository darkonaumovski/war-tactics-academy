# Leadership Lessons from Wellington: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 18 November 2026, for Volume 5 under `docs/LEADERSHIP_WELLINGTON_PROMPT.md`, after its studies phase (Assaye, Badajoz and Burgos, `48763c6`). It grades whether each planned lesson can be carried by sources actually opened, and traces every saying in the prompt's traps table. No lesson text exists yet, so nothing here is cited on the site; each batch re-reads what it cites and records it in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

**How the reading was done.** All primary texts were read as the Internet Archive's OCR text of University of Toronto scans, searched by keyword and then read in context, with page numbers taken from the running heads:

- **His own words.** Gurwood, _The Dispatches of Field Marshal the Duke of Wellington_, first edition (John Murray, 1834–1838). The scans are catalogued with wrong dates and some with wrong volumes (the scan labelled vol. I holds 1811), so each volume was identified from its title page: vol. V (1809), vol. VI (1810, title page MDCCCXXXVI), vols. IV, VII and VIII (searched only), vol. X (1812–13, MDCCCXXXVIII), vol. XI (1813–14) and vol. XII (1814–15, MDCCCXXXVIII).
- **Recorded conversation.** Philip Henry Stanhope, _Notes of Conversations with the Duke of Wellington, 1831–1851_ (1888); _The Croker Papers_, ed. Louis J. Jennings, 3 vols. (2nd ed., 1885); _The Creevey Papers_, ed. Sir Herbert Maxwell, vol. I (2nd ed., 1904).
- **Later witnesses.** Charles de Montalembert, _The Political Future of England_ (English translation, 1856); Sir William Fraser, _Words on Wellington_ (1889; Routledge reprint).
- **Narrative.** Oman, _A History of the Peninsular War_, vol. V (1914): the preface and the Salamanca chapters, pp. iii–iv and 418–474.
- **Psychology**, as abstracts only (PubMed and OpenAlex): Lerner and Tetlock (1999), a review of accountability research; McNatt (2000) and Kierein and Gold (2000), meta-analyses of the Pygmalion effect in organisations; Jussim and Harber (2005), a review of expectation effects.

Already read for the catalog's studies in earlier sessions, and to be re-read in the batch that cites them: Oman vols. III, V and VI; the 1911 Britannica "Peninsular War", "Assaye" and "Wellington"; Britannica's Wellington biography (Elizabeth Longford) and "Battle of Vitoria"; the Fondation Napoléon's Wellington entry (Bertier de Sauvigny); Gurwood vols. II and IX; Sardesai and Grant Duff on the Maratha side; Lamare and Marabel Matos on Badajoz; Larpent's journal.

Identified but not read: Gurwood's enlarged edition (1844–1847) and the _Supplementary Despatches_; Napier; Fortescue; Foy's _Vie militaire_ (no scan found on the Internet Archive, so Foy's diary is known only as Oman translates it); Marmont's and Jourdan's memoirs; any Portuguese or Spanish historiography; modern scholarship (Muir, Esdaile, Davies), none of it openly readable so far.

Grades: **strong** means the lesson's main claims and its counter-evidence are in hand. **Adequate** means the argument can be made but named gaps remain. **Thin** means the lesson must be redrawn, merged or dropped.

## Corrections to the plan

1. **Gurwood's edition is evidence of choice.** It blanks names (the officer blamed for Burgos), leaves correspondents anonymous ("To — Esq." for the 1815 letter on Waterloo), and prints an English translation of the 1810 proclamation with the note that it is not the original draft. Lessons cite the edition as the record Wellington allowed, not as his unmediated voice.
2. **The 1810 devastation has two documents, and the first is harsher.** The order to Cotton threatens hanging for magistrates who stay and inhabitants who communicate with the enemy; the proclamation threatens trial as traitors. Both are dated 4 August 1810. Lesson 10 (new numbering) uses both, with the date.
3. **Gurwood also prints the French reply.** Masséna's proclamation to the Portuguese, in French, follows Wellington's: France comes as a friend, and Britain uses Portuguese battalions as a rampart and will leave when it suits her. It belongs in lessons 8 and 10 (new numbering), paraphrased, as the other side's charge.
4. **The Lines were also an exit.** The memorandum of 20 October 1809 names the embarkation of the British troops in case of reverse as an object connected with holding Lisbon. Lesson 1 uses it for the arithmetic of one army; lesson 2 for the preparation.
5. **His own statement of method is the best evidence for the volume's question, and it is late.** The 1852 reply to Croker (troops covered behind rising ground; attack at the moment the enemy is about to attack) was written thirty-seven years after Waterloo by a man defending his record. Lessons 2 and 4 use it as his account of himself.
6. **"Scum of the earth" cuts both ways.** In the letter it is part of an argument about discipline and the regulations, written after the plunder at Vitoria. In Stanhope he adds that it is wonderful they were made into fine fellows. Lesson 7 (new numbering) quotes both and the editorial rule about context applies.
7. **"The nearest run thing" comes with a figure and a boast.** Quote only the phrase; Creevey's sentence gives losses in numbers. Creevey also records Wellington saying the battle would not have been won had he not been there, and says he left that out of his letter home as unfair to the Duke. Both belong in the record of how the Waterloo story was made.
8. **The other side of the hill is a late recollection.** Croker wrote it down in September 1852, recalling a remark made about thirty years earlier on a coach journey. It is not a battlefield saying.
9. **The Eton saying is a French writer's anecdote published after his death,** without the playing fields. Ban it, and let the ledger show how a legend grows.
10. **The Salamanca epigram is unattributed in Oman,** and Oman's own narrative undercuts it as a measurement.
11. **Foy is known only through Oman.** His _Vie militaire_ was not found as a scan. Cite the diary as Oman translates it and gives its page in Girod de l'Ain's edition.
12. **Merge lesson 5 into lesson 4.** The only French witness read on Wellington's reputation is Foy's diary, which belongs with Salamanca. Jourdan at Vitoria can enter lesson 4 if Oman VI supports it. The volume then has twelve lessons, numbered 0–11.
13. **Counter-case for the breach: Napoleon at Acre** (`acre`), whose assaults failed and whose own record of it the Napoleon volume already uses. Alexander at Tyre (`tyre`) is the alternative if the lesson needs a storm that succeeded and was followed by killing.
14. **Burgos is the failure the volume needed, and the letter to Liverpool is his account of it.** Larpent records the argument at headquarters over who was to blame; Oman disputes the letter. Lessons 5 and 9 (new numbering) divide this: the cost in lesson 5, the reporting in lesson 9.
15. **Vimeiro, Cintra and the inquiry stay in prose** with no source read beyond Stanhope's recollections of Roliça and Vimeiro. Lesson 9 (new numbering) must find a source or keep them to a sentence.
16. **Fraser's Uxbridge story is a weak witness.** Published in 1889, it reaches Fraser through Vivian and Alava without saying how. Lesson 6 (new numbering) may tell it only with that chain.
17. **No Portuguese or Spanish source has been read.** Rule 10 asks for both traditions. Before lesson 10 (new numbering) a Portuguese account of 1810 must be found, or the lesson says what it cannot see.
18. **No figures.** Creevey, Gurwood's appendices and Oman give numbers for armies and losses; lessons describe without them.
19. **The chance before the Lines came early** (added in batch V1). Oman's account of the opportunity Wellington refused against Junot at Sobral falls at the turn of October and November 1810 (vol. III, pp. 453–454), not after Masséna withdrew.
20. **"The first time he had ever attacked" is a diarist's error** (added in batch V1). Tomkinson's remark on Salamanca, as Oman quotes it, is contradicted by Assaye, where Wellesley's own memorandum explains why he attacked against imperfect information. Lesson 4 sets the two side by side.
21. **The arithmetic was partly imposed** (added in batch V2). Besides the Viseu letter, Wellington told Hill on 2 April 1810 that his instructions forbade any risk or unnecessary loss, and told his brother Henry on 11 June that the government wanted nothing done but what was safe and cheap (Gurwood VI, pp. 3–4, 180–181). Lesson 1 presents caution both as his calculation and as a rule he worked under, without choosing between them.
22. **Ciudad Rodrigo in 1810 is lesson 1's shadow** (added in batch V2). Oman III, pp. 241–254, gives Wellington's refusal to relieve the fortress, Herrasti's bitter report to the Spanish government and La Carrera's departure in anger; Wellington's letters of 11 and 20 June (Gurwood VI, pp. 180–181, 206) give his reasoning. Barclay's counter-case rests on the Fondation Napoléon's biography and the 1911 Britannica, both read in the batch.
23. **The Santander letter used is of 24 June 1813** (added in batch V2). The complaint that sea communication was insecure and that magazines had to come by sea or be bought with ready money is in the letter to Bathurst from Irurzun, 24 June 1813 (Gurwood X, pp. 458–459); the order to bring the Corunna ships to Santander is of 10 June (p. 429). The Huarte letter of 2 July stays with lesson 7.
24. **The best-known Badajoz letter is missing from Gurwood's first edition** (added in batch V2). The letter to Liverpool that, by Wellington's note to Torrens, accounted for the loss could not be found when Gurwood compiled vol. IX; his footnote says only that its précis recommended a corps of sappers and miners (p. 49). Lesson 5 quotes the Torrens letter and the footnote, and nothing from the missing letter.
25. **The sack of Badajoz and the order to end it reach the lesson through Oman** (added in batch V2), who quotes the order of 7 April 1812 from the _Supplementary Despatches_ (Oman V, pp. 261–264); the _Supplementary Despatches_ were not read. Wellington's letter to Canning of 1820 on quarter for stormed garrisons is likewise quoted by Oman (pp. 259–260), from an article by Callwell, and is paraphrased.
26. **San Sebastián is seen from the British side only** (added in batch V2). Wellington's letter to the magistrates (Gurwood XI, pp. 246–247) and Oman VII, pp. 31–35, who dismisses the Spanish manifestoes, were read; no Spanish account was. Lesson 5 says so, and its source check leaves the worst charges open.
27. **Acre's counter-case uses the entry of 10 May 1799,** not the note of 9 May that the Napoleon volume already quotes, and carries Johnston's warning that his diary abbreviates and transposes words and dates (_The Corsican_, preface and pp. 98–99).
28. **Oman reads the Coa as trust, not control** (added in batch V3). The note of 22 July 1810 to Craufurd was a suggestion, which Oman takes as a mark of Wellington's confidence (Oman III, pp. 258–259). Lesson 6 therefore shows both failures, of control and of trust. The note and the letter to Pole of 31 July are not in Gurwood's first edition and are quoted through Oman (p. 266); the dispatch to Liverpool is (Gurwood VI, pp. 279–280).
29. **A second blank in Gurwood** (added in batch V3). The memorandum of 13 August 1809 on an officer who acted without orders prints his name as a blank (Gurwood V, pp. 36–37). The lesson does not guess it.
30. **Vitoria's subordinates come from Oman VI** (added in batch V3): Dalhousie's discretionary orders and Picton's advance, pp. 408–411; the three generals who left their route in the Burgos retreat, pp. 151–152, whom Oman identifies against Fortescue as Stewart and probably Oswald and Dalhousie. Lesson 6 names none of the three. Graham's caution at Vitoria was not read and is not used.
31. **Fraser names no informant and the scan gives no date** (added in batch V3). The Uxbridge story (pp. 1–3) is told without its source; the Routledge reprint read is undated, so the lesson does not date the book.
32. **Kierein and Gold (2000) is not in PubMed and was not read** (added in batch V3). Lesson 7 rests its psychology on McNatt (2000) and Jussim and Harber (2005), both abstract only. Creevey's record of Wellington pointing at a private soldier in Brussels (vol. I, p. 228) adds the care side.
33. **Accounts of the decision to retreat on Wavre differ** (added in batch V3). Teske (NDB) has Gneisenau order it in Blücher's sense; Hardinge, as Stanhope records him in 1837, says Blücher and Grolmann overruled Gneisenau's doubts (Stanhope, pp. 109–110). Lesson 8 separates the order from the argument and its source check turns on the difference.
34. **Lesson 8's counter-case is Schwarzenberg** (added in batch V3), from the Fondation Napoléon's biography and the 1911 Britannica, both read in the batch; Foch is left to the comparison page. Masséna's proclamation of 1810 (Gurwood VI, pp. 309–311) cites Ciudad Rodrigo against the British. No Spanish account of Cuesta or of the command of 1812–1813 was read, and the lesson says so.
35. **The Britannica claim that the 1811 evacuation confirmed his soldiers' trust was not re-read** (added in batch V3) and is not used.

## Psychology to read in the batches

- Accountability (lessons 1 and 9): Lerner and Tetlock (1999), abstract only. It reviews when accountability reduces, leaves alone or amplifies biases, and whether it changes thought or only what people say. The limit: laboratory and organisational studies, no commanders, and lesson 1 and lesson 9 must not repeat each other's use.
- Expectation effects (lesson 7): McNatt (2000), 17 studies, and Kierein and Gold (2000), 13 effect sizes, both finding stronger effects in military settings and with people of whom little was expected; Jussim and Harber (2005), finding classroom effects typically small and expectations often accurate. `base: "contested"`. Wellington's case is low expectations stated in private letters and conversation, which the soldiers did not hear; the limit must say so.
- Lesson 4 links the Napoleon volume's intuition lesson rather than restating it.
- Lessons 0, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 11 plan `base: "none"` unless a sound source is found.

## Wellington's profile: proposal for a later batch

The profile (`arthur-wellesley`, `concise-profile`) cites only the National Army Museum. Proposed revision, in its own batch after the pilot and before integration:

- **Source.** Britannica's biography by Elizabeth Longford, re-read in the browser, as the profile's source; the NAM page and the 1911 Britannica article as checks. The Fondation Napoléon entry adds the French view.
- **Summary, approach, strength, limitation, legacy** rewritten to the depth of Blücher's profile: India (Seringapatam, Mysore, the Deccan, Assaye), the Peninsula from Vimeiro to Toulouse, 1815, and the political career in one clause where it bears on his command.
- **Principal commands.** "British forces in India, 1797–1805" overstates it: he commanded in Mysore and in the Deccan under his brother as governor-general. Add the command of the Spanish armies granted by the Cortes in 1812.
- **Timeline.** Add his supersession after Vimeiro, the Convention of Cintra and the inquiry; Badajoz (1812); the invasion of France and Toulouse (1814). Keep "Iron Duke" out of the profile's own prose.
