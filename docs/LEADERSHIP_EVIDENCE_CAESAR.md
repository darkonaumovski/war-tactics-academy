# Leadership Lessons from Julius Caesar: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 20 November 2026, for Volume 6 under `docs/LEADERSHIP_CAESAR_PROMPT.md`, after its studies phase (Bibracte, the Sabis, Gergovia, Ilerda, Dyrrhachium and Munda, `902ad1d`). It grades whether each planned lesson can be carried by sources actually opened, and traces every saying in the prompt's traps table. No lesson text exists yet, so nothing here is cited on the site; each batch re-reads what it cites and records it in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

**How the reading was done.** Classical texts were read in the Loeb translations on LacusCurtius (penelope.uchicago.edu), chapter by chapter, with the Latin pages checked where a Latin phrase was at issue:

Edwards), books 1–7: 1.1–29 and 1.39–41 (the Helvetii; the panic at Vesontio), 2.16–28 (the Sabis), 3.18 (the Venelli), 4.13–15 and 4.25 (the Usipetes and Tencteri; the landing in Britain), 5.28–30 and 5.52 (Sabinus and Cotta; the relief of Quintus Cicero), 6.30, 6.35 and 6.42 (fortune), 7.28 (Avaricum) and 7.34–54 (Gergovia). _Civil War_ (A. G. Peskett) 1.37–87 (Ilerda) and 3.41–75 (Dyrrhachium).
- **His officers' and continuators'.** Hirtius, _Gallic War_ 8, the preface to Balbus and 8.44 (Uxellodunum); _The Spanish War_ (A. G. Way), chs. 27–32 with Way's notes and introduction.
- **Later writers.** Suetonius, _Julius_ (J. C. Rolfe), 25, 30–34, 36–37, 45, 56–58, 65–70, 74 and 82, with the Latin of 32 and 37; Plutarch, _Caesar_ (B. Perrin), 10–11, 15, 17–18, 20, 22, 32, 36, 39, 50–51 and 56, and _Pompey_ 60 and 65–66; Appian, _Civil Wars_ 2.42–43, 2.60–63 and 2.92–94; Cassius Dio 38.31–33, 39.1–3, 40.34–38, 41.20–23, 41.47–51, 42.52–55 and 43.35–40; the elder Pliny, _Natural History_ 7.91–94, in Latin on LacusCurtius and in Rackham's English on Wikisource.

Already read for the catalog's studies, and to be re-read in the batch that cites them: the Alesia and Pharsalus studies' sources (_Gallic War_ 7.68–90 on Perseus and LacusCurtius, the Livius Pharsalus article with McDevitte and Bohn's _Civil War_ 3.88–99, Plutarch's _Pompey_, Dio on Pharsalus, the Alésia MuséoParc); the Teutoburg, Cannae, Zama, Actium and Burgos studies' sources for the counter-cases.

Identified but not read: Cicero's letters in Shuckburgh's translation (on Perseus, `Perseus:text:2007.01.0041`; Project Gutenberg was down for maintenance during the pass, and the clemency letter of Caesar to Oppius and Balbus was not located); Cicero's _Brutus_ (on Perseus in Latin only), known here only through Suetonius's quotation; the _Alexandrian_ and _African War_; Velleius Paterculus and Florus (both on LacusCurtius); Lucan; Augustus's _Res Gestae_ (on LacusCurtius); _Civil War_ 1.1–36 (the outbreak, Corfinium) and 3.1–40 and 3.76–112; _Gallic War_ 7.1–33 and 8.1–43 beyond the passages named; modern scholarship (Meier, Gelzer, Goldsworthy, Raaflaub), none of it openly readable so far.

Grades: **strong** means the lesson's main claims and its counter-evidence are in hand. **Adequate** means the argument can be made but named gaps remain. **Thin** means the lesson must be redrawn, merged or dropped.

## Shape

The profile already links all eight studies. Counter-case profiles named by the curriculum all exist (`hannibal-barca`, `alexander-the-great`, `pompey`, `napoleon-bonaparte`, `gebhard-leberecht-von-blucher`, `august-von-gneisenau`, `arminius`, `augustus`, `arthur-wellesley`), as do the linked lessons (`tempo-and-the-decisive-point`, `the-king-in-the-front-rank`, `opis`, `a-short-leash`, `what-people-will-do-for-recognition`, `your-rivals-are-learning`, `the-government-at-home`) and exercises (`two-fronts-at-alesia`, `pompeys-cavalry`).
- **Question.** What won Caesar his wars: his speed, his soldiers, his enemies' divisions, or his own account of them? The pass found two ancient verdicts that frame it from opposite ends. The elder Pliny ranks him first for a vigour and speed of mind "winged as it were with fire", then refuses to count to his glory the number he killed in battle outside the civil wars, calling it a great wrong to mankind (_Natural History_ 7.91–92, Rackham's translation). Asinius Pollio, who served under him, judged that the _Commentaries_ were put together carelessly and without strict regard for truth (Suetonius, _Julius_ 56.4). The orientation should state both as claims to test.
- **Twelve lessons**, from the prompt's thirteen, with lesson 7 merged into lesson 5 (correction 9). Parts unchanged.

## Corrections to the plan

1. **The later books were not demonstrably written by his officers.** Suetonius 56.1 says the author of the _Alexandrian_, _African_ and _Spanish War_ is unknown, some think Oppius, others Hirtius; Hirtius's preface says he supplemented the _Gallic War_ and completed Caesar's unfinished work from Alexandria to the end of his life, though he was not present in Egypt or Africa; Way calls the author of the _Spanish War_ unknown. The orientation says so.
2. **Cicero's praise of the _Commentaries_ reaches this pass through Suetonius.** Suetonius 56.2 quotes the _Brutus_ (memoirs "naked in their simplicity", meant as material for historians). The _Brutus_ itself was found only in Latin on Perseus and not read. Lesson 0 quotes it as Suetonius gives it.
3. **Pollio's judgement is narrower than "without regard to truth".** Suetonius 56.4: put together somewhat carelessly and without strict regard for truth, because Caesar believed others' reports too readily and misreported his own actions designedly or from forgetfulness, and Pollio thought he meant to revise them. Suetonius 30.4 also makes Pollio the witness of Caesar's words at Pharsalus, and Plutarch 32.7 places him at the Rubicon.
4. **The village is in the Alps, and the story has a sequel.** Plutarch 11 sets it on the way to Spain after his praetorship, and follows it with his tears over Alexander's achievements; the lesson that uses one should know of the other.
5. **"Men believe what they wish" is about an enemy.** _Gallic War_ 3.18 explains why the Venelli fell for Sabinus's ruse; it is Caesar's observation of others, not a confession.
6. **Napoleon's _Précis_ exists and is readable, in French OCR.** It gives a sharper hostile verdict on 55 BCE than the prompt expected and a list of Caesar's defeats that omits Gergovia, which Suetonius 25 counts among only three reverses in Gaul. Lessons 0, 3, 4 and 11 (new numbering) may use it.
7. **Lesson 3 has a third explained defeat.** At the relief of Quintus Cicero, Caesar told the army to bear the loss at Aduatuca, incurred through the fault and foolhardiness of a general, with the more equanimity (5.52). Gergovia blames the soldiers, Aduatuca a lieutenant, Dyrrhachium fortune and anyone rather than himself (_Civil War_ 3.73). The lesson sets the three side by side.
8. **Lesson 3's counter-case: Napoleon,** reading Caesar's defeats in the _Précis_ and writing his own bulletins, linking the Napoleon volume's orientation. Pompey is kept for lesson 9 (new numbering), so he is not the counter-case twice.
9. **Merge lesson 7 into lesson 5.** Both rest on the same evidence (the rewards in Suetonius and Plutarch, the mutiny of 47 and its unpaid promises), and an army bound by pay and plunder is lesson 5's claim. The cost of the rewards to Gaul moves to lesson 8 (new numbering, clemency set against the massacres) and the ledger. The volume then has twelve lessons, numbered 0–11.
10. **Lesson 4's counter-case: Wellington at Burgos** (`burgos-1812`), a siege lifted when relief armies converged, with its causes in the study's sources (Oman VI, Wellington's letter to Liverpool, Larpent). Vicksburg is the parallel success, if one is wanted.
11. **The mutiny of 47 has four incompatible versions.** Suetonius and Plutarch tell of a single word; Appian of a discharge and a theatrical relenting; Dio of a calculated disbandment and of the bold men used up in Africa. Lesson 5 compares them and does not choose.
12. **Plutarch does not mention Gergovia**, and the Arverni's sword hung in a temple belongs, in Plutarch 26, to the fighting before Alesia (found in Phase S).
13. **Pliny's verdict cuts both ways.** The same chapters praise his speed of mind and his clemency and refuse to count his killings as glory. The ledger uses all three.
14. **The psychology is mostly already used.** Leader prototypicality is in `opis` and the Napoleon volume; legitimacy (Tyler) in the Alexander volume; after-action reviews and extrinsic rewards elsewhere. Lessons 5 and 10 (new numbering) link those lessons rather than restate them. Only lesson 3 brings a new review (Mezulis and others, 2004).
15. **The Gallic side has no voice of its own in anything read.** Every Gallic speech is Caesar's, Dio's or Plutarch's composition; archaeology (Bibracte, Gergovie, Alésia) gives places, not words. Lessons 7 and 8 (new numbering) say so.
16. **Figures throughout.** Pliny's count, Plutarch's sums for the soldiers, Caesar's numbers at Alesia, the Helvetii and the Usipetes, and Napoleon's own estimates are left out; the lessons describe without them.

## Psychology to read in the batches

- Self-serving attribution (lesson 3): Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde and Hankin (2004), a meta-analysis of 266 studies, abstract only. It finds the bias large and nearly universal but smaller in Asian samples and varying with age. The limit: everyday attributions measured in modern samples, while Caesar's explanations were public reports written to persuade; the lesson can say only that the pattern is common, not that he believed his own account. Ellis and Davidi (2005), a quasi-field experiment with soldiers, found that reviewing successes as well as failures improved performance; usable as a single study, if the lesson needs the learning side, without repeating the debrief meta-analyses already cited.
- Third-person self-reference (lesson 0): Kross and others (2014) was read and is not used. It shows that non-first-person self-talk helps people regulate stress during private introspection; Caesar's third person is a literary convention for public readers, and the study cannot speak to it.
- Legitimacy (lesson 10, new numbering): Tyler (2006), abstract only, already used in the Alexander volume; only with a different question and a link, or `base: "none"`.
- Identification with a leader (lesson 5): Hogg and others (2005) is a single pair of surveys, not a review, and the reviews (Hogg, van Knippenberg and Rast; Steffens and others) are already used in `opis` and the Napoleon volume. Link them; `base: "none"`.
- In the new numbering, lessons 0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 11 plan `base: "none"` unless a sound source is found.

## Caesar's profile: proposal for a later batch

The profile (`julius-caesar`, `concise-profile`) cites Jona Lendering's Livius article, which was not re-read in this pass, and was written before the studies phase. Proposed revision, in its own batch after the pilot and before integration:

- **Source.** The 1911 Britannica's "Caesar, Gaius Julius" (read for Phase S) and the Livius article, re-read; Britannica's modern biography, read in the browser, if it opens.
- **Strength and limitation.** The strength names only the works at Alesia. Add the speed his contemporaries and Pliny remarked on, and the bond with his army; the limitation keeps Gergovia and Dyrrhachium and adds the Usipetes and Tencteri, with Cato's proposal (Plutarch 22) and Napoleon's verdict.
- **Legacy.** Keep the violence of the conquest; add Pliny's refusal to count the killing as glory, without the number, and the _Commentaries_ as the record most of his wars are still read through.
- **Timeline.** Add the Sabis (57), Ilerda (49), Dyrrhachium (48), Zela (47) and Munda (45). The setback entry should quote no motive he did not give.
