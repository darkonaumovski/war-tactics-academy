# Leadership Lessons from Grant and Lee: evidence pass

Phase 0 record, 23 November 2026, for Volume 8 under `docs/LEADERSHIP_GRANT_LEE_PROMPT.md`, after its studies phase (Fort Donelson, Shiloh, Chancellorsville, the Wilderness, Spotsylvania and Petersburg, `789e748`). It grades whether each planned lesson can be carried by sources actually opened, and traces every saying in the prompt's traps table. No lesson text exists yet, so nothing here is cited on the site; each batch re-reads what it cites and records it in `docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md`.

Grant gives the route because the volume asks why he won; Lee's profile leads to the same page. Title: "Leadership Lessons from Grant and Lee: the American Civil War, 1861–1865". Parts: Orientation; Grant in the West; Lee in Virginia; Grant against Lee; The end and after.
- **Question**, unchanged: Why did Grant win: persistence, the Union's resources, Lee's own choices, or does the record decide what we see? The pass found the two verdicts that frame it. The 1911 Britannica writes that Grant would have followed other unsuccessful generals into retirement had he not shown, "whatever his mistakes or failures, and whether he was or was not sober and temperate in his habits", the iron determination Lincoln and the Northern people wanted; Pryor writes that Lee's hands-on leadership was one reason he frustrated Grant's powerful machine for so long, and that historians have questioned whether his costly offensives were the right strategy. Persistence and Lee's choices are both on the table from the start.
- **Thirteen lessons**, as planned. Lesson 9 is thin and must be read up or merged (correction 14); lessons 6 and 10 are adequate with named gaps.
- **Eight studies** are available to the lessons: the six of Phase S with Vicksburg and Gettysburg, plus the Atlanta campaign for Sherman.

## Corrections to the plan

1. **The fight-it-out sentence.** Grant wrote "purpose", not "propose" (ch. LII; 1911 Britannica). The traps table is corrected here, and `lib/validation.ts` rejects the "propose to fight it out" form.
2. **"So terrible".** The earliest text read is Cooke's (1871), and it reads "this is so terrible", not "war is so terrible"; Cooke names no hearer. The prompt's form is banned.
3. **"It's all my fault".** Fremantle's words are longer and were addressed to Wilcox; the prompt's contraction is banned. Fremantle wrote within months and was present, so his is a witness's report; his preface says he kept the diary day by day and published it for friends in England eager for the truth about the South, whose people and leaders he openly admired. The lesson should name that sympathy.
4. **The "butcher" origin is still open.** Neither source read says who first used the word. Lesson 7 may use the label only as reported, or the batch must read further.
5. **Lee did not criticise Longstreet in writing**, according to Piston; lesson 5 should say so before presenting the postwar charge.
6. **Grant's verdict on the cause.** At Appomattox he calls the Confederate cause "one of the worst for which a people ever fought, and one for which there was the least excuse" (ch. LXVII). It belongs in lessons 0 and 11 as his judgement, with the declarations as the evidence.
7. **The 1911 Britannica's "Lee" never mentions slavery,** and quotes, in its own quotation marks, the phrase that his army "carried the rebellion on its bayonets". It is a specimen of the record, useful in lesson 0, and should not be cited as a neutral account of Lee.
8. **The Norris testimony is quoted through the Park Service.** The National Anti-Slavery Standard itself was not read; the lessons quote Norris as the Park Service prints him and say so.
9. **Lee's slaveholding** has two parts that must not be confused, as the Park Service warns: the few people he inherited and owned, and the much larger number he managed as executor of the Custis estate. Give no counts.
10. **Grant's colored division.** In the Wilderness, Grant writes, Burnside's colored division was sent to guard the wagon train and he did not see it again until July (ch. L); at the Crater, Grant writes that Meade "interfered" with Burnside's choice of it, and Greene gives Meade's reasons. Lesson 10 can carry both.
11. **Counter-cases proposed** (all with catalog profiles and studies):
    - Lesson 1: Oskar Potiorek in 1914, whose invasions of Serbia outran his means (`oskar-potiorek`), or Crassus at Carrhae.
    - Lesson 2: Gerd von Rundstedt in June 1944 (`gerd-von-rundstedt`, `normandy`), surprised by the landings and unable to recover.
    - Lesson 3: link the Hannibal volume's `over-the-alps` and Napoleon in 1812, as planned.
    - Lesson 4: Napoleon dividing his army with Grouchy in 1815 (`emmanuel-de-grouchy`, `waterloo`), where the divided wing failed to return.
    - Lesson 5: Ludendorff's offensives of 1918 (`erich-ludendorff`), which spent an army Germany could not replace; Wellington's `the-only-army` linked.
    - Lesson 6: Conrad von Hötzendorf (`franz-conrad-von-hotzendorf`), directing armies on several fronts without the means or the allied agreement to make them press together; or Napoleon in 1813.
    - Lesson 7: Haig on the Somme (`douglas-haig`), linking the Western Front's `the-ledger-of-learning`; the Hannibal volume's `the-delayer` for the opposite method.
    - Lesson 8: Tadamichi Kuribayashi on Iwo Jima (`tadamichi-kuribayashi`), who held fortified ground without hope of supply until his garrison was destroyed; Putnik's retreat linked.
