# War Tactics Academy

**The Art of Command — Learn from History. Think Like a General.**

[![Deploy to GitHub Pages](https://github.com/darkonaumovski/war-tactics-academy/actions/workflows/deploy.yml/badge.svg)](https://github.com/darkonaumovski/war-tactics-academy/actions/workflows/deploy.yml)

**Live site: [darkonaumovski.github.io/war-tactics-academy](https://darkonaumovski.github.io/war-tactics-academy/)**

A historical learning application about military history, command decisions and their consequences. It combines a battle atlas, commander profiles, wars and campaigns, a decision simulator and a structured academy. It is built with Next.js, React, TypeScript, Prisma and SQLite, and is published as a fully static site on GitHub Pages. No login, paid service, remote database or API key is required.

The guiding principle is to explain what commanders knew, what they decided and why, and to keep historical fact, scholarly interpretation, uncertainty and counterfactual reasoning clearly apart.

## Features

### Historical catalog

- **178 engagements** across seven eras, classified as battles, sieges, operations or campaigns, with approximate locations. Operation Overlord (Normandy) is the first operation record.
- **178 detailed studies**, one for every engagement in the catalog, each with 14 analysis sections and an interactive replay of four or more phases. All 2,492 sections show which listed sources they draw on, with book and chapter locators for classical texts and chronicles and section names for handbooks.
- **384 command perspectives** across all 178 engagements. Each covers objective, available information, uncertainty, constraints, forces, terrain, logistics, decisions and retrospective interpretation, clearly presented as reconstructed reasoning rather than quotations.
- **132 commander profiles**, each with a command role (ruler-commander, field, naval, staff planner, or coalition and theater commander), ranks and titles, principal commands, a dated chronology, a notable decision, defeats and reverses, linked engagements and further reading. Commanders with two or more linked engagements get a career strip (one dot per engagement on a year axis, with a band for each war) and a dated list of those engagements with their outcomes. The catalog filters by era and command role, and any two commanders can be compared side by side without numerical ratings.
- **43 war pages** with principal participants, geographic scope, a chronology of key developments, key commanders and reading from linked studies and profiles; each links to its campaigns and engagements. Campaign pages draw a schematic map with the engagements numbered in date order (a locator map when a campaign has one engagement); the line joins them in that order and does not trace any army's movements. All 101 campaign pages add sourced text: a narrative for the sixty-six campaigns of the collections (the four of Alexander, the sixteen of Rome, the thirteen of Napoleon, the fifteen of the First World War, including the four of the Serbian Front, and the eighteen of the Second World War) and a short context for the thirty-five campaigns outside them. The Alexander, Roman, French Revolutionary, Napoleonic and both world war pages add an annotated war bibliography.
- **Six collections** (Alexander, Rome, Napoleon, the First World War, the Serbian Front and the Second World War), each with an introduction, a strategic arc that links the studies it summarises, a map of its engagements numbered in date order (joined by a line for Alexander and the Serbian Front), and a dated timeline. Membership follows wars and campaigns, so new studies join their collection automatically.

### Interactive learning

- **Battle replay** with pan, zoom, phase selection, playback, event popups, a legend and a full text timeline. Replay diagrams are labeled as schematic, never as surveyed troop positions.
- **Offline world atlas** using bundled Natural Earth geography; no map tile server is needed. Nearby markers merge into numbered groups that zoom in when selected, a region select frames dense areas such as the Balkans or northern Italy, markers that never separate fan out, and `/atlas?battle=<slug>` opens the map on one engagement.
- **Forty-two decision exercises** (Waterloo, Cannae, Midway, Austerlitz, Alesia, Hastings, the Hydaspes, Pharsalus, Auerstedt, Jutland, Leyte Gulf, the Teutoburg Forest, Borodino, the Marne in 1914, Market Garden, and six on the Serbian Front: Cer, the crossing into Syrmia, the Kolubara, the strike on Bulgaria proposed in 1915, the retreat of 1915 and Dobro Pole, and four from Napoleon’s campaigns written for the Leadership Lessons: raising the siege before Castiglione, stopping at Smolensk, the road home from Maloyaroslavets and the bridge at Leipzig, two from the Western Front written for the Leadership Lessons: breaking off at Amiens and the armistice terms at Senlis, three from Alexander’s campaigns written for the Leadership Lessons: besieging Tyre, Parmenion’s night attack before Gaugamela and turning back at the Hyphasis, three from Wellington’s written for the Leadership Lessons: leaving Spain after Talavera, relieving Ciudad Rodrigo and the noon attack at Salamanca, and three from Caesar’s written for the Leadership Lessons: the surprise before leaving Gergovia, leaving the coast after Dyrrhachium and starving out the Pompeians at Ilerda, and three from the Second Punic War written for the Leadership Lessons: Hannibal after Trasimene, Nero before the Metaurus and Scipio’s choice of Africa in 205, and three from the American Civil War written for the Leadership Lessons: Grant after the first day at Shiloh, Lee on the evening of 2 July at Gettysburg and Grant after the Wilderness): read the situation, choose under uncertainty, then compare with what happened and with an explicitly counterfactual interpretation.
- **Ten learning paths with 51 lessons**: Foundations of Military History, Classical Warfare, Alexander's Campaigns, 334–325 BCE, Rome at War, 197 BCE–451 CE, Napoleonic Warfare, Napoleon's Campaigns, 1796–1815, Industrial and World Wars, The First World War, 1914–1918, The Second World War, 1939–1945, and The Serbian Front, 1914–1918. Each lesson has objectives, historical cases, a concept diagram and a quiz; the Classical, Alexander, Rome, Napoleon, Industrial, First and Second World War and Serbian Front lessons add readings and seminar prompts.
- **Leadership Lessons from Napoleon**: nineteen lessons, each testing one conditional claim against his career through separate historical, strategic and psychological readings, with a failure case, a counter-case, the human cost, a quiz and a seminar prompt. The volume page sets each lesson’s strength beside its shadow, commander profiles and engagement studies link to the lessons that cite them, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question: did he get worse, or did the problem get harder?
- **Leadership Lessons from Alexander the Great**: thirteen lessons in the same form, for a commander whose own record is lost. Each lesson names the chain of witnesses behind its episodes (Arrian and his sources, the vulgate, Plutarch, a Babylonian astronomical diary, the later Persian traditions) and treats the speeches in the ancient histories as their authors’ compositions. Counter-cases range from Darius III and Hannibal to Napoleon at Acre, Pétain in 1917 and Tokugawa Ieyasu, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question: did his army stop following him, or did he stop leading it?
- **Leadership Lessons from the Serbian Supreme Command, 1914–1918**: twelve lessons in the same form for a shared command, Radomir Putnik with Stepa Stepanović, Živojin Mišić and Petar Bojović, from Cer and the Kolubara through the retreat of 1915 to Dobro Pole. Every lesson asks who actually decided, the volume is linked from all four commanders’ profiles, and the human cost includes typhus, the occupation, the Toplica uprising and the Salonika trial. Counter-cases range from Potiorek, Conrad and Hindenburg to Kutuzov, Napoleon at the Berezina and Ludendorff in 1918, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question: did its commanders make Serbia’s endurance, or did they keep from wasting it?
- **Leadership Lessons from Foch and Haig: the Western Front, 1914–1918**: twelve lessons in the same form for a volume shared by Douglas Haig and Ferdinand Foch, asking whether the generals learned and whether the learning was worth its price. It is built on the commanders’ own published records, Haig’s despatches and Foch’s memoirs and lectures, read against encyclopedias, museum histories and open scholarship, and it separates what was planned from what was later said to have been planned. Lessons run from the Somme plan and Passchendaele through Doullens, Amiens and the Hundred Days to the armistice terms; counter-cases include Ludendorff, Brusilov, Currie, Pershing, Monash and Hindenburg, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question and counts who paid, among them the soldiers executed and pardoned only in 2006.
- **Leadership Lessons from Wellington**: twelve lessons in the same form on Arthur Wellesley as a commander from India to Waterloo, asking whether caution can be a form of daring. It is built on his own dispatches in Gurwood’s edition, read as the record he allowed, and on conversation recorded by Stanhope, Croker and Creevey, each with its recorder and date; banned sayings that cannot be traced are kept out by the lesson schema. Lessons run from the Lines of Torres Vedras and Salamanca through the storms of Badajoz and Burgos to the court of inquiry into Cintra, and the human cost includes the Portuguese population ordered from their homes in 1810, read through Portuguese historians as well as British and French papers, the sacks of stormed towns and the Maratha war of 1803. Counter-cases include Masséna, Barclay de Tolly, Napoleon at Acre and in Russia, Blücher and Gneisenau, Schwarzenberg, Kutuzov and Marlborough, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question and enters the costs beside the victories.
- **Leadership Lessons from Julius Caesar**: twelve lessons in the same form on Caesar in Gaul and in the civil war, asking what won his wars: his speed, his soldiers, his enemies’ divisions, or his own account of them. It reads his _Commentaries_ as a record written while he fought, for readers in Rome who would judge him, beside Hirtius, the unknown author of the _Spanish War_ and the later lives and histories of Plutarch, Suetonius, Appian and Cassius Dio, in the Loeb translations on LacusCurtius, and it says plainly how little Gallic testimony survives; untraced or misattributed sayings are kept out by the lesson schema. Lessons run from speed and the front rank at the Sabis through his defeats at Gergovia and Dyrrhachium, the siege lines of Alesia, his lieutenants, Gaul divided and his clemency to Pompey and the Rubicon, and the human cost includes the Usipetes and Tencteri, Avaricum, Uxellodunum and the prisoners of Alesia given to his soldiers as plunder. Counter-cases include Hannibal, Gustavus Adolphus, Napoleon, Alexander at Opis, Gneisenau, Arminius, Augustus, Pompey and Wellington, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question and enters the costs beside the victories.
- **Leadership Lessons from Hannibal and Scipio: the Second Punic War, 218–201 BCE**: thirteen lessons in the same form for a volume shared by two opposing commanders, asking why the better tactician lost the war: Hannibal’s strategy, Rome’s system, Scipio’s command, or the record written by the winners. It reads the war as one recorded by the winners’ friends: Polybius, who drew on Scipio’s friend Laelius and was the friend of Scipio’s adoptive grandson, Livy two centuries later, Appian and Plutarch (Polybius and Plutarch in the Loeb translations on LacusCurtius, Livy in Canon Roberts’s translation on Wikisource, Appian in Horace White’s on Livius), with Hannibal’s own historians surviving only in his enemies’ quotations. Lessons run from the invasion of Italy, reading the Roman consuls, the allies who stayed with Rome and an army of many peoples, through Fabius’s delay, the young Scipio’s command, learning from the enemy, allies made and kept and the debate over Africa, to the meeting at Zama and what both states did with their great men. The human cost includes Italy laid waste by both armies, Capua and Tarentum, Locri, New Carthage, the Africans sold after Baecula and Carthage razed in 146 BCE. Counter-cases include Caesar, Pompey, Napoleon, Marmont, Darius III and Zhukov, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question and invites the opposite argument.
- **Leadership Lessons from Grant and Lee: the American Civil War, 1861–1865**: thirteen lessons in the same form for a volume shared by the two opposing commanders of the war’s last year, asking why Grant won: persistence, the Union’s resources, Lee’s own choices, or a record that decides what we see. It reads a war whose record the losers rewrote: Grant’s _Personal Memoirs_, finished days before he died, set against the Lost Cause that made Lee its emblem, with Lee’s reports and letters, Fremantle’s, Cooke’s and Chamberlain’s accounts, the 1911 Britannica, the Tennessee and Virginia encyclopedias and National Park Service histories, and the war’s cause stated from the Confederate states’ own declarations of secession. Lessons run from unconditional surrender at Fort Donelson, the surprise at Shiloh and cutting loose at Vicksburg, through Lee’s divided army at Chancellorsville and his third day at Gettysburg, to Grant as general-in-chief, persistence from the Wilderness to the James, Lee’s irreplaceable army at Petersburg, the halted prisoner exchange, whose war it had become, and Appomattox. The human cost includes the enslaved people the Confederacy fought to keep, the soldiers of both armies and the wounded who burned in the Wilderness, the Black soldiers killed after surrendering at Fort Pillow, prisoners on both sides, and the civilians of Vicksburg and Petersburg. Counter-cases include Caesar, Potiorek, Rundstedt, Napoleon, Ludendorff, Haig, Kuribayashi, Richard I and Hannibal, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question and invites the opposite argument.
- **Leadership Lessons from William Slim: from Gallabat to Rangoon, 1940–1945**: fourteen lessons in the same form on the commander who led a beaten army out of Burma in 1942 and back to Rangoon in 1945, asking how an army that has learned to lose is rebuilt: by leadership, by means, through the enemy’s mistakes, or through a record that decides what we see. Slim’s own books are in copyright, so it reads him through those who quote him, beside Kirby’s British official history, the Indian official histories of East Africa and Arakan, the despatches of Wavell, Alexander, Giffard and Leese, the Japanese army’s postwar monographs and a Japanese defence historian, the U.S. Army’s histories, and soldiers’ recollections recorded decades later; the Kohima Epitaph without its author, an untraced saying and the wartime slur for the Japanese are kept out by the lesson schema. Lessons run from his first defeat at Gallabat, the retreat of 1942 and the first Arakan, through the rebuilding, the Admin Box and the soldiers of an army of many peoples, to Imphal, Mutaguchi’s gamble, Wingate, Burma last in line for Allied means, Meiktila and the attempt to replace him after Rangoon. The human cost includes the refugees of 1942, the Bengal famine at the army’s base, the wounded killed at the Admin Box, the Naga people of the Kohima hills, the prisoners and labourers of the Burma–Siam railway and the Japanese soldiers who starved at Kohima and the Sittang. Counter-cases include Grant’s memoir, Potiorek, Napoleon’s retreat from Moscow, Longstreet, Wavell at Donbaik, Stalingrad, the defeat of 1942 that raised the Indian National Army, Singapore, Midway, Kawabe, Rommel, Moltke, Belisarius and Kimura, and the last lesson answers the volume’s question and invites the opposite argument.
- **The volumes compared**: a page that sets eleven problems recurring across the eight volumes (reading the record, taking over an organisation, presence, reading the enemy, dissent, when to stop, what cannot be replaced, succession, allies and coalitions, victory and settlement, and who pays) beside the lessons that test them, with each lesson’s claim, strength and shadow and a note reading across the volumes, labelled as interpretation and drawn only from those lessons. Lesson pages link to the problems they are compared under.
- **Curated media library** of nine verified records: two official National Archives (UK) D-Day videos, loaded only on request through privacy-enhanced YouTube embeds; public-domain portraits of Grant and Eisenhower, also shown on their profiles; two historic maps; and three primary documents. Each record stores publisher, license, attribution and verification date.
- **Search and filters**: global search across battles, commanders, wars, campaigns, collections, lessons and leadership lessons. Engagements are found by name, place, date, war, campaign, commander or collection; every word of a query must match, in any order, ignoring case and accents, and exact titles rank first. Commanders filter by era and command role. The battle catalog filters by era, war, campaign, commander, collection, region and category, sorts by date or name, and switches between cards and a compact list; every filter is kept in the address, so a filtered view can be shared, and war, campaign, commander and collection pages link to their filtered catalog.
- **Saved progress** in the browser: bookmarks, recently visited records, lesson scores, decision choices and the light or dark theme. No account is needed.

### Design and accessibility

- Editorial dark interface with an optional light theme, a desktop sidebar and mobile navigation.
- Self-hosted typography: Cormorant Garamond for headings, Source Serif 4 for reading text, Inter for the interface and IBM Plex Mono for labels and coordinates.
- Subtle motion: home-page sections fade up as they scroll into view, and new timeline phases and quiz or decision feedback ease in. Nothing is hidden or moved for readers who prefer reduced motion, and content is visible without JavaScript.
- Every page has its own title, description, canonical URL and link-preview (Open Graph and Twitter) metadata, and a [sitemap](https://darkonaumovski.github.io/war-tactics-academy/sitemap.xml) lists all content pages.
- Keyboard navigation, a skip link, semantic landmarks and automated accessibility checks on 19 pages in the dark theme and 6 in the light theme.

## Quick start

Prerequisites: Node.js **22.12 or later** (tested with Node 24.20.0) and npm. SQLite is embedded; no separate database server is needed. Internet access is needed to install packages and browsers and to download fonts during the first build.

```sh
git clone https://github.com/darkonaumovski/war-tactics-academy.git
cd war-tactics-academy
npm ci
npm run db:setup
npm run dev
```

Open **http://127.0.0.1:3000**. In PowerShell environments that block npm's `.ps1` shim, use `npm.cmd` instead of `npm`.

The default database is `prisma/academy.db`. Copy `.env.example` to `.env` only to change it:

```env
DATABASE_URL="file:./prisma/academy.db"
```

`npm run db:setup` generates the Prisma client, applies the checked-in migrations and seeds the catalog. After pulling changes, run it again (or `npm run db:migrate` followed by `npm run db:seed`). The seed is transactional and idempotent: it updates existing records, replaces each record's source links and removes campaigns no longer in the data; other removed records are not pruned automatically. Browser progress is independent of the database.

## Production build and deployment

```sh
npm run build
npm start
```

`npm run build` reads the seeded catalog once and prerenders every page (480 pages, the not-found page and `sitemap.xml`) into `out/`. `npm start` serves `out/` on http://127.0.0.1:3000 with `scripts/serve-static.mjs`, which behaves like GitHub Pages: folder paths resolve to `index.html`, paths without a trailing slash redirect, and unknown paths return the custom `404.html` with a 404 status. The deployed site needs no server or database.

### GitHub Pages

The site is live at **[darkonaumovski.github.io/war-tactics-academy](https://darkonaumovski.github.io/war-tactics-academy/)**. [`.github/workflows/deploy.yml`](.github/workflows/deploy.yml) runs on every push to `main`. It installs dependencies, sets up and seeds the database, runs lint, type checking and unit tests, builds with `BASE_PATH=/<repository-name>/` and `SITE_URL` set to the Pages address, and publishes `out/`. If any check fails, the previous version stays live. Progress is visible in the repository's [Actions tab](https://github.com/darkonaumovski/war-tactics-academy/actions).

To set this up in a fork: Settings → Pages → Source: "GitHub Actions". On a free GitHub plan, Pages requires a public repository.

To preview the Pages URL layout locally:

```sh
BASE_PATH=/war-tactics-academy/ npm run build
BASE_PATH=/war-tactics-academy/ npm start
```

Then open http://127.0.0.1:3000/war-tactics-academy/. In Git Bash on Windows, prefix both commands with `MSYS_NO_PATHCONV=1` so the path is not rewritten.

Static hosting shapes a few details: URLs end with a trailing slash; search terms and battle filters in the query string (for example `?war=napoleonic&view=list`) are applied in the browser; the video tab is its own page (`/library/videos/`); portraits load directly from the holding archive because there is no image optimizer; and catalog changes reach the site only through a new build.

## Historical methodology

Historical fact, scholarly interpretation, uncertainty and counterfactual analysis are explicitly distinguished. Detailed studies cite institutional histories and primary texts, with caveats about retrospective testimony and conflicting evidence. The 165 studies are concise editorial syntheses, not full scholarly monographs.

Study sections name the listed sources they draw on. These references were assigned only after each source page had been read; sources that could not be opened are listed but not attached to individual sections. They are section-level references, not footnotes for every sentence. Commander chronologies and war overviews summarize well-established events; their further reading is not a citation for each entry. All source links were checked, and dead links were replaced with verified pages.

No casualty totals, private thoughts, invented quotations, precise intelligence logs or commander performance ratings are fabricated. Geographic markers show approximate places. Replay geometry is an abstract teaching schematic, not verified troop coordinates or surveyed movements. Political and civilian consequences, including atrocities, remain part of the historical context.

See the [source audit](docs/SOURCE_AUDIT.md) and [commander sourcing notes](docs/COMMANDER_SOURCES.md). Specialist review and fuller contrasting bibliographies remain important next steps.

## Media and licensing

Map art, monograms and diagrams are original SVG graphics. Monograms are graphic identifiers, not likenesses; only commanders with an item-level public-domain portrait record show a photograph. Natural Earth world geography is public domain, distributed through `world-atlas`; [Natural Earth terms](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/about/terms-of-use/) apply.

The four typefaces are licensed under the SIL Open Font License. `next/font` downloads them at build time and serves them from the site, so visitors' browsers never contact Google. Only the Latin files (about 84 KB) are preloaded; other character ranges, such as Vietnamese, load only on pages that use them.

External readings remain with their publishers; no publisher artwork or full article is copied. Curated media lives in `data/media.json`, is validated by `lib/media.ts` and is also seeded into the `MediaResource` table with its source, publisher, license and attribution. Video IDs were checked against the publisher's own page and YouTube oEmbed; nothing is autoplayed or rehosted. Portraits load directly from the holding archive and fall back to a text notice with attribution if the image cannot load. Historic maps and documents are linked, not reproduced. See [media provenance](docs/MEDIA_RESEARCH_UPDATE.md).

## Known limitations

- The ten learning paths are introductory editorial courses (one question per lesson), not reviewed university curricula.
- The 178 battle analyses and 132 commander profiles need deeper scholarly review, fuller biographies and sentence-level citations before being treated as an academic reference work. Study sections are still brief.
- War pages give orientation, not comprehensive operational histories; only the Alexander, Roman, French Revolutionary, Napoleonic and world war pages have a dedicated bibliography. The thirty-one one-engagement campaigns outside the collections have only a short context.
- Replay diagrams explain abstract relationships; they do not simulate combat or reconstruct exact formations. Atlas locations and Natural Earth coastlines are approximate modern context.
- The media library is a small starting collection (nine records); the two videos cover only D-Day. Embeds and archive images depend on third-party availability.
- Progress belongs to one browser and is lost if its storage is cleared; storage failures are reported without crashing.
- The site is static: the database is a build-time content store and updates require a rebuild. Accounts, synchronization, a historian-facing editor and sentence-level citation workflows are future work.
- Automated accessibility checks supplement, but do not replace, manual screen-reader testing. Lighthouse has been run on the live site and, after the payload work, on a local build (see the verification record); the battle catalog remains the slowest of the pages measured on phones.
